# X.509 Certificates & PKI

Zero Trust demands strong device identity. The gold standard for that is the **X.509 certificate**, underpinned by **PKI** — the same system that secures HTTPS, now securing IoT devices.

## The problem: how do you trust a public key?

Public-key cryptography lets a device prove its identity with a **private key** (kept secret) and a **public key** (shared). But how do you know a given public key *really* belongs to the device claiming it, and not an impostor? You need a trusted third party to **vouch** for it. That's what a certificate does.

## X.509 certificates

> [!NOTE]
> An **X.509 certificate** is a standard digital document that **binds a public key to an identity** (a device, server, or person), **signed by a trusted Certificate Authority (CA)**. It's the cryptographic "ID card" a device presents to prove who it is.

A certificate's main components:

| Field | Holds |
|---|---|
| **Subject** | Who the certificate identifies (the device/owner) |
| **Public key** | The subject's public key |
| **Issuer** | The **CA** that signed it |
| **Validity period** | Not-before / not-after dates |
| **Serial number** | Unique ID for the certificate |
| **CA's digital signature** | The CA's signature binding it all together |

## PKI — Public Key Infrastructure

> [!NOTE]
> **PKI (Public Key Infrastructure)** is the system of CAs, policies, and procedures that **issue, manage, validate, and revoke** certificates — the framework that makes certificates trustworthy at scale.

Key roles: the **Certificate Authority (CA)** (issues/signs certificates), the **Registration Authority** (verifies identity before issuance), and **revocation** mechanisms (**CRL** / **OCSP**) to invalidate a compromised certificate before it expires.

## Digital signatures

> [!NOTE]
> A **digital signature** proves a message's **authenticity and integrity** and provides **non-repudiation** (Unit 4's security goals). The signer **hashes** the data and **encrypts the hash with their private key**; anyone can verify it with the signer's **public key**.

> [!INTUITION]
> Signing is **encrypting a fingerprint with your private key**. Only *you* have the private key, so only you could have produced the signature (authenticity + non-repudiation); and since it's a hash of the data, any change breaks it (integrity). A CA signs a certificate exactly this way — vouching "this public key really belongs to this device."

## The chain of trust

> [!NOTE]
> Trust flows in a **chain (trust chain)**: a **Root CA** (self-signed, implicitly trusted) signs **Intermediate CAs**, which sign **device/end-entity certificates**. To trust a device certificate, you verify each signature **up the chain to a root you already trust**.

> [!INTUITION]
> The chain is like **nested vouching**: you trust the device because an intermediate CA vouches for it, and you trust that intermediate because the **root CA** vouches for it — and the root is one you trust outright (it's pre-installed in your trust store). Verification walks **up** the chain to that anchor. Break or distrust any link and the device's identity can't be verified. This is exactly how your browser trusts a website — and how AWS IoT/Azure trust a device.

## Why this matters for IoT

Certificate-based identity (often **mutual TLS**, where *both* device and server present certificates) is the **strongest practical device authentication** — far better than shared passwords (OWASP #1). It's how cloud platforms (AWS IoT Core, Azure IoT Hub) authenticate devices, and the foundation of Zero Trust's "verify identity, not network."

> [!EXAM]
> Know an **X.509 certificate** binds a **public key to an identity, signed by a CA** (components: subject, public key, issuer, validity, CA signature); **PKI** is the CA framework that issues/manages/**revokes** (CRL/OCSP) certificates; a **digital signature** = hash encrypted with the private key (gives authenticity, integrity, non-repudiation); and the **chain of trust** (root CA → intermediate → device, verified up to a trusted root). It's the strongest IoT device authentication (mutual TLS) and the basis of Zero Trust.
