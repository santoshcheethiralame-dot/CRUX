# A Brief History of AR

## The six milestones

The course names six turning points. Learn **year → name → the *first* attached to it**.

### 1968 — Ivan Sutherland's Head-Mounted Display

> Known as the **"Sword of Damocles"**, this was the **first AR system using an HMD** connected to a computer to **overlay simple wireframe graphics onto the real world**. Though **primitive and limited by the technology of the time**, it marked the **conceptual beginning of augmented reality**.

> [!NOTE]
> The name comes from the apparatus: the headset was so heavy it had to be **suspended from the ceiling** above the user, hanging over them like the sword in the Greek legend. It rendered wireframe cubes and tracked head position mechanically — and it is simultaneously the first AR system *and* the first VR system, because the distinction did not yet exist.

### 1992 — the term "Augmented Reality" is coined

> Introduced by **Tom Caudell**, a researcher at **Boeing**, while developing a digital display system to **assist workers in assembling complex aircraft wiring harnesses**.

*(The course's Unit 1 treatment credits **Caudell & Mizell** jointly — both attributions are correct; Mizell was his co-author.)*

> [!INTUITION]
> It is worth noticing **why** the term was born at Boeing. Wiring a plane means following diagrams for thousands of near-identical cables. The idea was to **project the diagram onto the workpiece** so the technician never looks away. AR was invented as an **industrial instruction problem**, not as entertainment — and industry remains its most commercially successful application.

### 1993 — KARMA

> **KARMA (Knowledge-based Augmented Reality for Maintenance Assistance)** was an early application of AR in **industrial maintenance**. It provided **knowledge-based guidance** by overlaying information about equipment repairs and maintenance tasks directly onto the physical components.

*(Developed by **Feiner** and colleagues at Columbia; the classic demonstration guided a user through servicing a laser printer.)*

### 1997 — The Touring Machine

> The **first outdoor mobile AR system**, combining a **head-mounted display with GPS and sensor data** to overlay navigational information and annotations on the surrounding environment as the user moved outdoors.

> [!NOTE]
> Also from Feiner's group at Columbia. Moving AR **outdoors** is a genuine step change: you lose the controlled lighting, the prepared environment and the fixed tracking rig, and must rely on GPS and inertial sensors — which is why the mobile-sensor limitations in a later topic matter so much.

### 2003 — Invisible Trains on PDAs

> An early example of **handheld AR gaming**, allowing players to control **virtual trains overlaid onto a physical track layout** viewed through a **PDA's camera feed**.

> [!INTUITION]
> The significance is the **form factor**. Until now AR meant a headset tethered to a workstation. Invisible Trains showed AR working on a **device people already carry**, using its **camera as the see-through mechanism** — the model every phone AR app still uses.

### 2008 — Smartphone AR with natural feature tracking

> The introduction of **Vuforia**, one of the first platforms to enable **natural feature tracking on smartphones**, marked a **turning point for AR's commercial adoption**. It allowed mobile applications to **recognize and track images or objects in the environment** and overlay digital content on them in real time.

> [!INTUITION]
> The technical leap is *natural feature* tracking. Before this, AR needed **fiducial markers** — printed black-and-white patterns placed in the scene. Vuforia could track **ordinary images and objects**, so no one had to prepare the environment first. That is the difference between a laboratory demonstration and something you can ship to a million phones. The mechanism is the subject of Unit 4.

## The arc

```
 1968 ─────── 1992 ─── 1993 ─── 1997 ─────── 2003 ─── 2008 ────────▶
 first HMD    named    first    first        first     first
 (lab,        (Boeing, industrial outdoor    handheld  practical
 tethered)    industry) app     mobile       (PDA)     smartphone
                                                       tracking

 ├──── laboratory ────┤├── industrial ──┤├── mobile ──┤├─ consumer ─┤
```

> [!EXAM]
> *"Trace the historical development of augmented reality"* is a standard 5–6 marker. Give the six milestones **in chronological order**, one clause each, and finish with a sentence naming the trend: **laboratory → industrial → mobile → consumer**, driven by tracking getting cheaper and less dependent on a prepared environment. That closing sentence is what separates a list from an answer.

> [!TRAP]
> The Unit 1 treatment lists **ten** milestones (adding 1994 UNC medical, 1996 Studierstube, 1997–2001 Japan's Mixed Reality Systems Laboratory, and 1998 ARQuake); Unit 3 lists **six**. They do not contradict each other — Unit 3 is a subset. If a question asks for "the historical milestones", giving the ten-item list is safe; if it asks for a *brief* history, six is enough.
