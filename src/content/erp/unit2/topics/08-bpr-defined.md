# Business Processes & Defining Reengineering

## What a business process is

> [!EXAM]
> **Definition:**
>
> **A business process is a set of logically related tasks that use the resources of an organization to achieve a defined business outcome.**
>
> - **A process has a clear beginning and an end, and clearly identified inputs and outputs**
> - **Processes are cross-functional and not restricted within a single function**

> [!EXAM]
> **Business process principles — the four:**
>
> 1. **It has a number of steps**
> 2. **Each step has specific input and output**
> 3. **Each step can be run by a separate organizational entity / department**
> 4. **Each step should add value to the whole process**
>
> **Consider any business as a well-coordinated combination of business processes.**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Principle 4 — "each step should add value" — is the one that makes BPR possible**, and it should be read as a **test you can apply**.
>
> If a step adds no value, it is a candidate for elimination. That single principle generates the whole **Value Added / Non-Value Added / Waste** classification in the diagnosis phase, and hence most of BPR's savings.
>
> **Principle 3** matters for a different reason: because each step *can* be run by a different department, a process **crosses departmental boundaries** — which is precisely why no department owns the outcome and why Unit 1's *function-to-process* driver was needed.
>
> The deck's note adds the economic argument: *"this keeps repeating over and over again, so many times — hence its importance. **We get the best value for money by focusing on improvements in steps that are repetitive.**"* A one-second saving on a step performed a million times a year is worth more than an hour saved on something done annually.

> [!EXAM]
> **Why is BPR in this course? "BPR is the third step of our ERP implementation methodology."**
>
> Recall Unit 1: **Business Process Re-engineering is an activity inside Phase 3, Business Blueprinting**, sitting between the As-Is model and the To-Be process.

## Hammer's definition of reengineering

> [!EXAM]
> **Reengineering — definition by Michael Hammer:**
>
> > **"A fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of business processes to achieve dramatic improvements in critical, contemporary measures of performance — such as cost, quality, service, and speed."**
>
> Learn it verbatim. The four named measures — **cost, quality, service, speed** — carry marks.

## The three important concepts

> [!EXAM]
> **There are three important concepts in Reengineering:**
>
> | Concept | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **Fundamental rethinking** | **BPR is not about doing small changes; it is all about a new way of thinking to do the process** |
> | **Radical redesign** | **The new process should look very different from the old one** |
> | **Dramatic improvements** | **A reengineered process can bring significant improvement in process performance in terms of cost, time or service performance** |

> [!INTUITION]
> **Mnemonic — BPR is FRD: Fundamental, Radical, Dramatic.**
>
> The three words are not synonyms — they describe **three different stages** of the same effort, and each one is a different kind of claim:
>
> $$\underbrace{\textbf{Fundamental}}_{\text{how you } think} \rightarrow \underbrace{\textbf{Radical}}_{\text{what you } design} \rightarrow \underbrace{\textbf{Dramatic}}_{\text{what you } achieve}$$
>
> **Thinking → design → result.** If a proposed change is not fundamental in its thinking, it will not be radical in design, and it will not produce dramatic results — it will be incremental improvement, which is **TQM**, not BPR.
>
> That chain is also the standard exam question *"how is BPR different from process improvement?"* answered in one line.

> [!TRAP]
> **The deck explicitly flags that Hammer's definition is dated**, and saying so earns credit:
>
> > *"Of course, the definition is 'dated'. Since modern businesses are already on good business practices, the terms **radical & dramatic may have to be toned down**."*
>
> The reasoning is that Hammer wrote in the early 1990s, when many large organisations were running processes that had never been systematically examined — so there was enormous slack to reclaim. A company already running ERP best practices has far less headroom.
>
> This is also why the BPR-and-ERP topic recommends a **"middle path"** rather than full-blown BPR, and why the deck notes elsewhere that *"many implementations driven purely by BPR have failed."*

## Reengineering as a discipline

> [!INTUITION]
> The deck draws an analogy worth carrying:
>
> > *"Reengineering in business is similar to reengineering software systems. That is, start with an existing process, review its performance and the current experiences, dwell on the current set of problems or 'opportunities for improvements', explore possibilities or imperatives for changing the business."*
>
> The parallel holds well. Software reengineering also begins with **understanding what exists** (As-Is), identifies **what is wrong**, and produces a **redesign** — and it also carries the risk of a rewrite that is worse than what it replaced.
>
> The deck adds the standing justification: **"even for a stable business, continuous transformation is required due to changes in the external environment"**, and notes that **"many organizational restructurings are driven by BPR."**

## A process spans departments

> [!EXAM]
> The deck's worked **Order Management Process**, showing the departments a single process crosses:
>
> | Department | Activity |
> |---|---|
> | **Sales** | Order entry · order pricing |
> | **Production** | Manufacturing items as per the order, giving them to Quality for final inspection |
> | **Quality** | Inspecting quality as per customer spec, moving to the finished goods warehouse |
> | **Warehouse** | Picking the item from finished goods stock |
> | **Distribution / Transport** | Delivering items from warehouse |
> | **Finance** | Invoicing · payment |

> [!DERIVE]
> **Because a business process spans departments, process execution efficiency depends on all the contributing departments.**
>
> That single sentence is the reason BPR must be **cross-functional** and why its team includes a **Steering Committee** to *"settle inter-process disputes."* No department can reengineer this process alone — Sales cannot fix a warehouse delay, and Warehouse cannot fix a pricing error made at order entry.
>
> It is also why the deck's process-modelling rules insist you **"assume no organizational structure or boundaries"** when mapping a process: the boundaries are the thing being questioned.
