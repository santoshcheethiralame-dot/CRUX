# Why BPR Fails

## The eight reasons

> [!EXAM]
> **BPR — Reasons for Failure:**
>
> 1. **Too much AS IS Analysis (Paralysis)**
> 2. **Poor Scoping**
> 3. **Ignore People Concerns**
> 4. **Not putting best people in the project**
> 5. **Little change in process — Not redesign**
> 6. **Looking for a sure success recipe** *(copy design from another successful BPR project, blindly)*
> 7. **Large scale implementation without prototyping**
> 8. **No Top Management Support**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Mnemonic — the eight sort into three families**, and naming the family lets you regenerate the members:
>
> | Family | Reasons | The error |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Analysis errors** | 1 · 2 | Studying the wrong amount, or the wrong things |
> | **People errors** | 3 · 4 · 8 | Wrong people, or the right people ignored |
> | **Design & execution errors** | 5 · 6 · 7 | The redesign isn't one, isn't yours, or isn't tested |
>
> **Analysis, People, Execution — A-P-E.** Note the **people family is the largest**, which is consistent with everything else in this unit: *organizational change is difficult because of "human" elements, mostly.*

## Analysis errors

> [!EXAM]
> **1 — Too much AS IS Analysis (Paralysis)**
> **2 — Poor Scoping**

> [!TRAP]
> **"Too much As-Is analysis" is a genuine trap because the As-Is model is a required deliverable.** The failure is not doing it — it is **over-doing it**.
>
> The BPM chapter states the correct depth explicitly: for As-Is modelling, *"**no need of much detailed analysis as anyway the process will not be used in future.** It is important to understand the process at high level — why the process exists, what it does, what's the high level objective."*
>
> Contrast with **To-Be modelling**, which *"needs to be detailed so that every step is clear to users."*
>
> **So the rule is: As-Is shallow, To-Be deep.** Teams get it backwards because the As-Is is the part they can actually observe, and documenting what exists feels like progress. It also connects to Unit 1's **"scope never gets finalized… analysis paralysis"** — the same pathology in the implementation phase.

> [!INTUITION]
> **"Poor scoping"** is the other side of the same coin. Scope too wide and you get paralysis; scope too narrow and the change cannot be **radical**, which lands you in failure reason 5.
>
> The process-selection phase is what sets this, using its three decision factors — **benchmarking, unsatisfactory/troubled processes, potential for quick results.**

## People errors

> [!EXAM]
> **3 — Ignore People Concerns**
> **4 — Not putting best people in the project**
> **8 — No Top Management Support**

> [!DERIVE]
> **Reason 4 is more subtle than it looks, and it has a cost attached.**
>
> Putting your best people on a BPR project means **taking them off their day jobs** — which is Unit 1's hidden cost: *"typically pulls out 'good' resources from other projects/functions"*, and *"team pull-back takes a long time."*
>
> So there is a real, immediate cost to doing it right, and a large, deferred cost to doing it wrong. Organisations under pressure staff the project with **whoever is available** — and the people who are available are, by definition, the ones their departments can spare.
>
> **The redesign is only as good as the people doing it**, and a process redesigned by people who do not deeply understand it will miss exactly the undocumented rules the diagnosis phase is supposed to surface.

> [!INTUITION]
> **Reason 8, "no top management support", is the one the deck emphasises most** — its note repeats across three consecutive slides: **"Leadership must believe in the new system."**
>
> Note the word **believe**, not *approve*. BPR makes existing jobs redundant and forces departments to surrender autonomy; those disputes can only be settled from above. An executive who has approved a budget but does not believe in the redesign will not spend political capital defending it when a department head objects.
>
> This is the same requirement as Unit 1's **"resolve issues of co-operation amongst seniors"** and Chapter 4's **sponsorship — "not just lip-service."**

## Design and execution errors

> [!EXAM]
> **5 — Little change in process — Not redesign**
> **6 — Looking for a sure success recipe** *(copy design from another successful BPR project, blindly)*
> **7 — Large scale implementation without prototyping**

> [!TRAP]
> **Reason 5 — "little change in process, not redesign" — is BPR failing by not actually being BPR.**
>
> Recall the three concepts: **Fundamental, Radical, Dramatic.** A project that tinkers has done **process improvement (TQM)** while paying BPR's costs — the disruption, the executive time, the resistance — without the dramatic upside that justifies them.
>
> The deck's proverb elsewhere is the sharpest statement of this:
>
> > **"Old process + new technology = costly old process."**
>
> Automating an unchanged bad process makes it **faster and more expensive**, not better.

> [!INTUITION]
> **Reason 6 — copying another company's successful design — fails for the reason benchmarking has to be handled carefully.**
>
> A redesign that worked elsewhere was fitted to **that** company's products, customers, staff and constraints. Copied blindly, it arrives without the reasoning that produced it, so nobody can adapt it when it does not fit.
>
> There is a deeper problem too. The competitive argument said organisations face *"dual pressures — to copy best practices and standardize, which may lead to homogenization, but at the same time be different and differentiated."* **A copied process cannot differentiate you** — by construction, someone else already has it. This is the same warning the BPR-and-ERP topic gives about ERP best practices: *"strong bias towards ERP driven best practice… may lead to losing competitive advantage."*

> [!EXAM]
> **Reason 7 — no prototyping — is the direct inverse of key-to-success #2**, *"prototype every process before large scale implementation."*
>
> The prototyping phase exists because *"though lot of thinking goes into creating a new process, **some unexpected issues might crop up while implementing**. So a prototype phase is a must to check viability and to some extent acceptance of the people involved. **Only after the prototype is run successfully can all the points be frozen.**"*

## Reading the list as a whole

> [!DERIVE]
> **A useful self-test: each failure reason has a matching countermeasure elsewhere in the chapter.**
>
> | Failure | Countermeasure |
> |---|---|
> | Too much As-Is analysis | As-Is at **high level only** *(BPM guiding principle)* |
> | Poor scoping | **Process selection** with its three decision factors |
> | Ignore people concerns | **Change Coordinator** role · proper change management |
> | Not best people | **Process Owner** *"obtains resources for the team"* |
> | Little change | **Fundamental, Radical, Dramatic** |
> | Copy blindly | **Key inputs to redesign** — mission, vision, values & culture |
> | No prototyping | The **prototyping phase** |
> | No top management support | **Leader** and **Steering Committee** roles |
>
> If you can produce this mapping, you can answer both *"why does BPR fail?"* and *"what are the keys to success?"* from the same memory.
