# The Need for BPR

## The five external forces

> [!EXAM]
> **BPR — need for:**
>
> 1. **External world keeps changing — business**
> 2. **External world keeps changing — technology**
> 3. **Components of business execution changing** — **people, machines, infrastructure**
> 4. **Business stakeholder expectations**
> 5. **Competition changes and challenges**
>
> > **Only thing that is constant in this world is change!**

> [!INTUITION]
> Four of the five are **outside the organisation's control** — business environment, technology, stakeholder expectations, competition. Only the third (people, machines, infrastructure) is internal.
>
> That asymmetry is the argument: **a process designed for one environment decays as the environment moves**, without anyone doing anything wrong. Nobody breaks the process; it simply stops fitting.
>
> The deck's note draws the practical conclusion:
>
> > **"Any organization which has been running stable for a while without much organizational changes is a potential candidate for BPR."**
>
> **Stability is the warning sign, not the reassurance** — which is a genuinely counter-intuitive point and a good exam line.

> [!EXAM]
> The deck's two concrete illustrations of environmental change:
> - **"Who sends an enquiry by post these days?"**
> - **"Customers expect to track the status of logistic processing of their order shipment"**
>
> Both are processes that were perfectly good and became obsolete **because the world changed around them**, not because they were badly designed.

## The competitive perspective

> [!EXAM]
> **BPR — need for — another perspective:**
>
> - **As businesses mature, a kind of uniformity / standardization sets in. Lowered differentiation** — everyone is looking at the same business pie
> - **To be in business:** **innovate models, break-through products/brands, and run processes better, faster and cheaper (than the competitor)**
> - **Modern trade and businesses across the globe open up competition like never before — no longer protected markets**
> - **Advancement in IT adaptations**

> [!TRAP]
> The deck names a real tension here that is worth quoting:
>
> > *"Businesses around the world have **dual pressures** — to copy best practices and standardize, which may lead to **homogenization**, but at the same time be **different and differentiated**."*
>
> This is the central paradox of the whole ERP course. Unit 1 sold **"brings best practices"** as benefit #1 — but if everyone adopts the same vendor's best practices, **nobody is differentiated by them.**
>
> The resolution appears in the BPR-and-ERP topic: adopt the standard practices for everything that is not your competitive advantage, and **protect the few processes that are**. As the deck puts it there: *"BPR team / implementers need to look for differentiators. Do not lose out on the company's special competence."*

> [!INTUITION]
> **"No longer protected markets"** is the historical driver. The deck's note explains: *"due to various governmental policies, ease of business in any part of the world has increased, so any good business at any part of the world can be replicated and competed with ease. Almost every country has rules against monopoly."*
>
> For an Indian student this is the post-1991 liberalisation story directly — domestic firms that had never faced global competition suddenly did, and processes built for a protected market stopped being adequate.

## Where BPR came from — downsizing

> [!EXAM]
> **BPR — Beginnings:**
> - The **strategic planning function** in an organization constantly reviews and rethinks **all aspects of business**
> - **In the 90s, one of the key changes recommended and followed in many organizations was "Downsizing"**
> - **Industry analysts act as consultants**
>
> **However:**
> - **It was overly simplistic and not smooth**
> - **Led to dissatisfaction and disruption**

> [!DERIVE]
> **Why downsizing was tried, and why it failed** — the deck's notes give both sides, and the pair is a good exam answer.
>
> **The rationale:** *"over time, departments/functions gain non-productive activities and resources that do not add value"* — and *"if you constrain the resources, newer and innovative methods will be found out."* Cutting resources was expected to force efficiency.
>
> **The failure:** *"value of resources, especially **Human**, was under-estimated, and the indirect impact on company image and credibility."*
>
> So downsizing correctly diagnosed the problem — **accumulated non-value-adding activity** — and applied the wrong instrument. It removed **people** when the problem was **process**. Cut the headcount and the bad process remains, now performed by fewer, demoralised people.
>
> **That is exactly the gap BPR filled**: attack the process, and let the headcount follow from the redesign rather than lead it.

> [!EXAM]
> **BPR — Significance:**
> - **Experience in downsizing led to more mature "business restructuring plans"**
> - **Based on business processes with focus on leaner and efficient operations**
> - **IT became an important part of the plans**
> - **Performance optimized and at the same time flexible to keep pace with changes**
> - **Clear shifting of focus from performance of machines / material resources to human resources — moving towards knowledge & information based business structures**
>
> > **BPR creates "Infrastructure" for future business!**

> [!INTUITION]
> The deck's verdict on the downsizing era is nuanced and worth reproducing: *"though downsizing resulted overall negative in the short term, it **created 'opportunity' for drastic improvement**."*
>
> It established two things BPR needed: that **radical organisational change was possible at all**, and — through its failure — that **change had to be designed around processes and people**, not around headcount targets.
>
> Note the last shift: **from machines and materials to human resources, and to knowledge- and information-based structures.** That is what makes **IT** the primary enabler of BPR, and it is why the BPR-vs-TQM table lists *primary enabler: statistical control* for TQM but **IT** for BPR.

## BPR's principles

> [!EXAM]
> **BPR takes a different view than:**
> - **Economies of scale**
> - **Stable, large market**
> - **Optimize existing process thoroughly and reap benefits for years**
>
> **Emphasis is on:**
> - **Customer centric operations**
> - **Ability to change roles, structures, workflows regularly to align with the marketplace**

> [!TRAP]
> The deck's note is careful not to dismiss what BPR departs from:
>
> > *"**Economies of scale is a very important industrial engineering principle.** But looking at only that may not be best, and is definitely non-flexible and 'risky'. **You can optimize/improve anything almost forever.**"*
>
> The critique is not that economies of scale are wrong — it is that they assume a **stable, large market**, and that assumption has failed. Optimising one process forever produces an ever-better version of a process that may no longer be the right process.
>
> **"Ability to change… regularly"** is the replacement goal: not a perfect process, but a **changeable** one. Contrast the old view — *"reap benefits for years"* — with the new one, where the environment will not hold still for years.

## The headline results

> [!EXAM]
> Some **November 1999** headlines the deck quotes:
>
> - **"Wal-Mart reduces restocking time from 6 weeks to 36 hours."**
> - **"Hewlett Packard's assembly time for server computers touches new low — four minutes."**
>
> **The reason behind these success stories: Business Process Reengineering!**

> [!TRAP]
> The deck immediately adds a caution that is worth carrying into any exam answer about BPR's benefits:
>
> > *"Note, we should know how to read these business numbers — **they are true and false simultaneously**."*
>
> A restocking time of "6 weeks to 36 hours" is a real measurement of **one metric on one process** — and it says nothing about total cost, or what happened to the other processes, or whether the comparison is like-for-like. Headline BPR numbers are selected precisely because they are spectacular.
>
> This pairs with the **benchmarking** warning later in the chapter: *"any benchmarking has many known 'games' and pitfalls — comparing apples to oranges."* **Dramatic improvement is BPR's stated goal, which makes its reported numbers exactly the ones to read sceptically.**
