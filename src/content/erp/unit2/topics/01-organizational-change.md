# Organizational Change — Overview

## What changes when ERP arrives

> [!EXAM]
> **Organizational change means a new way of working.** Organizational projects may change:
>
> - **People & structures**
> - **Technology**
> - **Process**

> [!EXAM]
> **An organization is an integration of … People, Process & Technology** — and the goal of aligning all three is to **realize benefits**.

> [!INTUITION]
> This is the **People–Process–Technology triangle**, and it is the thesis of the whole unit. The deck spells out the reasoning:
>
> > *"Organization is nothing but people, infrastructure and activities/processes connecting it."*
>
> An ERP implementation is a **technology-driven change** — the new system is the primary change driver. But you cannot install only the technology:
>
> - **ERP systems build best practices regarding processes into the software**, so **new workflows need to be developed and implemented** *(the Process corner)*.
> - **Because the new technology affects the people in the organization, the elements of preparing the people for the change need to be addressed** *(the People corner)*.
>
> **Change one corner and you are obliged to change the other two.** Unit 1 called this a *socio-technical system*; this unit is where that claim gets operationalised.

## The Gartner figures

> [!EXAM]
> From a **Gartner Group survey of 186 ERP implementations**:
>
> - **< 25% deliver hard monetary benefits**
> - **70% of failed projects did not use performance metrics**
> - **27% did not measure** at all

> [!DERIVE]
> **Read the second and third figures together — they are the actionable ones.**
>
> **70% of *failed* projects did not use performance metrics**, and **27% did not measure at all**. That is a statement about **measurement**, not about technology: projects that never defined what success looked like disproportionately failed to achieve it.
>
> This connects straight back to Unit 1's **business case tracking** — *"though cost benefit analysis is done in the beginning, actuals might differ; need to track it at the right milestones."* A project with no metrics cannot notice it is drifting, cannot correct, and cannot prove it succeeded.
>
> **The first figure sets the stakes:** fewer than one in four implementations delivers **hard monetary benefit**. Set that beside Unit 1's Standish figure of **10% success** and the picture is consistent — ERP projects fail often, and they fail for reasons that are largely organisational.

> [!TRAP]
> **"Hard monetary benefits"** is doing careful work in that first statistic. It does **not** say 75% of projects fail outright — it says fewer than a quarter produce benefits you can put a number against.
>
> That is exactly the **tangible vs intangible** distinction from Unit 1's ROI chapter. Many projects deliver real intangible gains — better data, best practices, staff morale — that never appear as a rupee figure. **The measurement problem and the benefit problem are the same problem.**

## Why change is difficult

> [!EXAM]
> **Organizational change is difficult because of "human" elements, mostly.**
>
> The deck's own name for it: **"Human Inertia."**
>
> > *"Change is always challenging at both individual and organizational level."*
>
> **One way to do it right is to address not only technology, but processes and people.**

> [!INTUITION]
> **"Human inertia"** is a well-chosen phrase because inertia is not opposition. An object at rest is not *hostile* to being moved — it simply continues doing what it was doing unless a force acts on it.
>
> That reframes the whole problem. The people resisting an ERP rollout are usually not **against** the company; they are **continuing**, which is what people do by default. **Change management is the force you apply**, and like any force it must be **sustained** — which is why the next topics are about communication, training and incentives rather than persuasion in a single meeting.
>
> The Nestlé case from Unit 1 is exactly this: *"the new business process confused most of employees, then resistance grew into rebellion."* It began as confusion, not defiance.

## Summary of the chapter's argument

> [!EXAM]
> The deck's own closing summary:
>
> - **Success of ERP implementation depends on the change management**
> - **A structured approach is required** to handle the diverse changes expected in an ERP implementation
> - **Change management strategy is closely linked to organization design**
> - **Communication is quite important**

> [!INTUITION]
> Note the second bullet — **"a structured approach is required."** The chapter's position is not that change management is a matter of good intentions or leadership charisma.
>
> It is a **discipline with named roles (the change team), named activities (per project phase), and a named methodology (ASAP's six processes)** — precisely the same treatment the implementation itself got in Unit 1. **Change is managed as a project, not hoped for.**
