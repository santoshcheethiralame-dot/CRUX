# BPR — Pros, Cons & Keys to Success

## The pros

> [!EXAM]
> **BPR — Pros:**
>
> 1. **Enterprise Integration**
> 2. **Empowered Employee**
> 3. **No Handoff**
> 4. **Simplified Process**
> 5. **Less Coordination, Reconciliation**
> 6. **Reduce NVA, Eliminate Waste**
> 7. **Case Manager as SPOC**
> 8. **Effective Use of IT**

> [!INTUITION]
> The eight pros are **not independent** — most of them follow from one structural move: **removing handoffs.**
>
> $$\textbf{No handoff} \rightarrow \textbf{simplified process} \rightarrow \textbf{less coordination \& reconciliation} \rightarrow \textbf{reduced NVA}$$
>
> A handoff is where a process passes between departments, and every handoff needs a **queue, a check, and a reconciliation** afterwards — all of which are non-value-adding. Remove the handoff and all three disappear with it.
>
> **Case Manager as SPOC** is the mechanism that removes handoffs (one person owns the whole case), and **Empowered Employee** is what makes it possible (that person can decide without escalating). **Effective use of IT** is what lets one person hold information that previously required four departments.
>
> So the list is really: *one design idea (kill the handoff), the two things that enable it (empowerment, IT), and the four consequences.*

## The cons

> [!EXAM]
> **BPR — Cons:**
>
> 1. **High Failure Rate**
> 2. **Can take long time**
> 3. **Can consume a lot of organizational resources** — costs, executive time
> 4. **Can make existing jobs redundant**
> 5. **High Resistance to change**

> [!TRAP]
> **Cons 4 and 5 are cause and effect, and the causation is the uncomfortable part.**
>
> BPR **"can make existing jobs redundant"** — that is not an accident of implementation, it is what *"radical redesign"* and *"reduce NVA, eliminate waste"* mean when the waste is a person's job. Remove the handoffs and you remove the people who managed them.
>
> Therefore **"high resistance to change"** is entirely rational. Chapter 4's *"Will my job remain?"* is not paranoia in a BPR project — it is an accurate reading of the situation.
>
> **This is why BPR and change management are adjacent chapters**, and why *"proper change management"* is listed as a key to success rather than as a nicety.

> [!INTUITION]
> Set the pros and cons side by side and several pairs are **the same fact seen twice**:
>
> | Pro | Con |
> |---|---|
> | Simplified process, no handoff | **jobs made redundant** |
> | Enterprise integration | **consumes organizational resources** |
> | Dramatic improvement | **high failure rate** |
>
> The last pair is the important one. BPR aims at **radical** change, and radical attempts fail more often than incremental ones. **The high failure rate is the price of the dramatic upside, not a separate defect** — which is exactly the BPR-vs-TQM trade-off: *risk moderate* for TQM, **high** for BPR.

## The keys to success

> [!EXAM]
> **BPR — Keys To Success:**
>
> 1. **Leadership and top management commitment in the project**
> 2. **Prototype every process before large scale implementation**
> 3. **Show early success within the first three to six months**
> 4. **Results to be measurable**
> 5. **Communicate effectively to manage change**
> 6. **Proper change management**
>
> The deck's note: **"Leadership must believe in the new system."**

> [!DERIVE]
> **Each key answers a specific con or failure reason** — which is the most useful way to hold them:
>
> | Key | What it defends against |
> |---|---|
> | **Top management commitment** | *"No top management support"* — a named failure reason |
> | **Prototype every process** | *"Large scale implementation without prototyping"* — a named failure reason |
> | **Show early success in 3–6 months** | *"Can take long time"* — momentum decays |
> | **Results measurable** | Gartner: *70% of failed projects did not use performance metrics* |
> | **Communicate effectively** | *"High resistance to change"* |
> | **Proper change management** | *"Ignore people concerns"* — a named failure reason |
>
> **Four of the six map directly onto a stated reason for failure.** The keys to success and the reasons for failure are the same list read from opposite ends, which is why they appear on adjacent slides.

> [!TRAP]
> **"Show early success within the first three to six months"** is the key most often omitted, and it is a hard constraint rather than a morale exercise.
>
> BPR's own con is that it *"can take long time"*, and Unit 1's implementation challenge was *"long timeline — difficult to sustain momentum; benefits/results cannot be delayed beyond a point."* An initiative that shows nothing for two years **loses its sponsor**, and losing the sponsor triggers the first failure reason.
>
> Note the tension with *"fundamental rethinking"*: BPR is supposed to be radical and radical change is slow. **The resolution is sequencing** — pick a process with *"potential for quick results"* first, which is exactly one of the three stated decision factors in process selection.

> [!INTUITION]
> **"Results to be measurable"** connects the two chapters of this unit.
>
> Chapter 4 opened with the Gartner finding that **70% of failed projects did not use performance metrics** and **27% did not measure at all**. Here the same requirement returns as a BPR success factor.
>
> It matters more for BPR than for ordinary projects because BPR's defining claim is **"dramatic improvements."** Dramatic *compared to what?* Without a baseline measurement of the As-Is process, the claim cannot be made at all — which is one of the three reasons the diagnosis phase insists on **recording the time for each step**.
