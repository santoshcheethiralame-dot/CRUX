# Process Redesign Principles

## The ten principles

> [!EXAM]
> **Process Redesign — Principles:**
>
> 1. **SPOC for Process** *(single point of contact)*
> 2. **Combine several jobs into one**
> 3. **Multiple versions**
> 4. **Simplify process**
> 5. **Reduce audits**
> 6. **Workers make decisions**
> 7. **Coaches**
> 8. **Maximize VA** *(value added)*
> 9. **Parallelize work**
> 10. **Process teams**
>
> The deck's caution: **"Principles are fairly broad. Redesign needs good thinking, innovation, vision and practicality."**

> [!INTUITION]
> The ten principles are **each aimed at one of the diagnosed problems**, which is how to hold them:
>
> | Diagnosed problem | Principle that answers it |
> |---|---|
> | **Too many hand-offs** | **SPOC** · **combine several jobs into one** · **process teams** |
> | **Sequential execution** | **Parallelize work** |
> | **Too many audits / inspections** | **Reduce audits** · **workers make decisions** |
> | **Few value-adding steps** | **Maximize VA** · **simplify process** |
> | **No process ownership** | **SPOC** · **coaches** |
> | **One rigid process for all cases** | **Multiple versions** |
>
> **Diagnosis → principle is a direct mapping**, and it is why the phases run in that order.

> [!TRAP]
> **"Multiple versions" looks like it contradicts "simplify process"** — and resolving that is a good exam point.
>
> A single process forced to handle every case must include **every exception**, which makes it complex for everybody. **Multiple versions** means a fast simple path for the routine 80% and a fuller path for the complex cases.
>
> So each version is **simpler than the one-size-fits-all process it replaces**, even though there are now several. Simplicity is measured **per case**, not per process count.

> [!INTUITION]
> **"Workers make decisions" and "reduce audits" are the same move.**
>
> Audits and checks exist because the person doing the work is **not trusted to decide**. Push the decision down to them — with the information and authority to make it — and the checking step becomes unnecessary. That is the **empowered employee** in the pros list.
>
> This also explains **"coaches"**: if workers decide, managers stop being approvers and become **supporters**. The job changes from controlling to enabling, which is why *new roles and responsibilities* is a lever of redesign.

## The Case Manager

> [!EXAM]
> **Case Manager is a Single Point Of Contact (SPOC) for a Process.**
>
> The deck's worked credit example:
>
> | | Flow |
> |---|---|
> | **Before** | Credit request → **Financing** → **Credit checking** → **Approval** → **Issuance** → Credit decision |
> | **After** | Credit request → **Case manager** → Credit decision |

> [!DERIVE]
> **Four departments become one role.** Count what disappears with them:
>
> - **Three hand-offs** vanish — and with each, a queue, a status enquiry and a chance to lose the file
> - **Reconciliation between the four departments' records** is no longer needed
> - **The customer gets one person to ask**, instead of being told *"that's with credit checking now"*
>
> The deck's note explains the requirement on the role: **"SPOC acts as a good interface between parties and should be highly approachable and responsive."**
>
> **What makes it possible is IT.** One person can only do four departments' work if they can *see* everything those four departments used to hold — which is precisely the **integrated database** from Unit 1. **Case Manager is the human-facing consequence of enterprise integration**, and it is why *"effective use of IT"* is in the pros list.

## Hammer's process-centred organization

> [!EXAM]
> **Hammer's Process-Centered Organization** *(Hammer, Beyond Reengineering, 1996, p.126)*:
>
> > **There is a process owner for each process, and process owners depend on several centers of excellence for bringing competency into the process. Each such COE can be managed by a coach.**
>
> The diagram shows **processes running horizontally toward CUSTOMERS**, with **Owners** on the processes and **Coaches** heading vertical **centers of excellence**.

> [!INTUITION]
> This is a **matrix**, and the orientation is the message.
>
> **Processes run horizontally, pointing at the customer.** **Centers of excellence run vertically**, holding specialist skill. A person belongs to a COE for their *expertise* but works on a *process* that delivers value.
>
> Compare with the traditional functional organization, where the vertical departments were the whole structure and nothing pointed at the customer at all. **The functional dimension survives as a skills home, but it is no longer the axis of delivery.**
>
> Note the two role names: **Owner** for the process, **Coach** for the competence — matching the redesign principles *"process teams"* and *"coaches"*. The coach develops people; the owner delivers outcomes.

## Key inputs to redesign

> [!EXAM]
> **BPR — Key inputs to redesign:**
> - **Stated principles**
> - **External consultants**
> - **Organization mission, vision, values & culture**
> - **Possibility of prototype and results of it**
>
> On consultants: **"Insiders are always 'biased' by virtue of being in the system for long. So external involvement is always beneficial."**

> [!TRAP]
> **"Organization mission, vision, values & culture" is the input that prevents failure reason 6** — *copying a design from another successful BPR project blindly.*
>
> A redesign must fit **this** organisation's values and culture. A process that works in a company with a high-autonomy culture may fail in one built on hierarchy — the *"workers make decisions"* principle assumes a culture where that is acceptable.
>
> **The principles are universal; the design is local.** That is the difference between using principles and copying a design.

## The six levers of process redesign

> [!EXAM]
> **BPR — Levers Of Process Redesign:**
>
> 1. **Roles and Responsibilities** — change or new emerge
> 2. **Measurements and Incentives** — **at process level rather than individual functional**
> 3. **Information Technology**
> 4. **Organizational Structure** — **flattened hierarchy**
> 5. **Skills** — **multi-skilling**

> [!INTUITION]
> They are called **levers** for a stated reason: *"because big changes are expected in the stated areas above."* These are the handles you pull to move a process.
>
> The deck adds a crucial implementation note:
>
> > **"Mostly they are interdependent also. Because of this, one may have to switch over to the newly designed process in stages. That is: set the levers in 1-2-2 state, stabilize the system; shift to higher levels 3-4-4… stabilize there…"**
>
> **You cannot pull all the levers at once.** Multi-skilling requires training; flattened hierarchy requires the new roles to exist; process-level measurement requires the process to be running. Move them together, in **steps, stabilising between each** — which is the same staged logic as the **Rollout** deployment strategy.

> [!DERIVE]
> **Two levers are really Chapter 4's content appearing inside Chapter 5.**
>
> **"Measurements and incentives at process level rather than individual functional"** is the fix for the diagnosed problem *"wrong measurements — task performance rather than process performance"*, and it is precisely **organizational design**: *"new performance measures are developed that decide what behaviour the organization will promote."*
>
> **"Roles and responsibilities"** and **"organizational structure — flattened hierarchy"** are the same thing: *"new processes lead to new roles and responsibilities and a new organization structure."*
>
> **BPR redesigns the process; organizational design makes the organization able to run it.** Neither works alone, which is why these two chapters sit in one unit.

> [!EXAM]
> **The deck's additional redesign dimensions**, from its notes:
> - **Flexibility to change**
> - **Keep looking at it from the customer perspective**
> - **Reduce "post mortem" data collections**
