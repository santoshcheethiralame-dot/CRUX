# Process Selection & Diagnosis

## Selecting the process

> [!EXAM]
> **BPR — Process Selection phase:**
>
> - Start with a **list of high level processes** — **not all activities are processes**
> - **Typical high level processes:**
>   - **Order fulfillment Process (OFP)**
>   - **Procure to pay process (PTP)**
>   - **Demand generation process**
>   - **Product development process (PDP)**
>   - **Employee Hire to retire process**
> - **Select the ones that need re-engineering**

> [!EXAM]
> **Decision factors — the three:**
> 1. **Based on Benchmarking**
> 2. **Unsatisfactory / troubled processes**
> 3. **Potential for quick results**

> [!INTUITION]
> The three factors answer three different questions, and a good selection satisfies more than one:
>
> - **Benchmarking** → *"where are we worse than others?"* — an **external** yardstick
> - **Unsatisfactory / troubled** → *"where does it hurt?"* — an **internal** yardstick
> - **Potential for quick results** → *"where can we show something soon?"* — a **political** yardstick
>
> The third is not cynicism. It is key-to-success #3 — **"show early success within the first three to six months"** — made operational at the point where it can actually be arranged. You choose the first process partly because it can deliver visibly and fast, which buys the sponsorship needed for the harder ones.

> [!TRAP]
> **"Not all activities are processes"** is a short line worth taking seriously.
>
> A process has *"a clear beginning and an end, clearly identified inputs and outputs"* and is **cross-functional**. "Approving a leave request" is an activity; **"Hire to Retire"** is a process. Selecting an activity for BPR guarantees a narrow scope, which is failure reason 2 (*poor scoping*) and leads to failure reason 5 (*little change, not redesign*).
>
> Note the listed processes are the same **X-to-Y** processes from Unit 1 — P2P, O2C (order fulfilment), H2R. **The unit of BPR is the enterprise process, not the departmental task.**

## Diagnosis — the steps

> [!EXAM]
> **BPR — Process Diagnosis and Analysis steps:**
>
> - **Understand steps in the current process — inputs, outputs**
> - **Categorize the steps into Value Added, Non Value Added and Waste**
> - **Record** — **time for each step**, **how the information system is used for each step**
> - **Understand issues in the process**
> - **For each issue — examine the rules and assumptions behind it**

> [!EXAM]
> **Key analysis criteria:**
> - **Understand rules and assumptions**
> - **Understand input/output — assess their value to the end result, specially as seen by the customer**
> - **Identify key steps and their parameters**
> - **List specific issues / problems if any**

> [!INTUITION]
> **"Record the time for each step"** is the step that makes *dramatic improvement* provable rather than asserted.
>
> BPR's defining claim is a dramatic improvement in **cost, quality, service and speed** — and you cannot claim a speed improvement without having measured the original. This is *"results to be measurable"* (key to success #4) made concrete, and it is what the Wal-Mart *"6 weeks to 36 hours"* headline rests on.
>
> **"Examine the rules and assumptions behind each issue"** is the *fundamental rethinking* concept in practice. Most bad process steps exist because of a rule nobody has questioned — often a rule whose original reason has expired. Asking *why* is what separates redesign from tinkering.

## Value Added, Non Value Added and Waste

> [!EXAM]
> **Typical Non Value Adding (NVA) activities of a process:**
>
> 1. **Expediting**
> 2. **Storing items**
> 3. **Moving material** — in and out of the company and inside
> 4. **Checks & Controls in a process**
> 5. **Inspection**
> 6. **Reworking**
> 7. **Reconciliation**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Mnemonic — the seven NVAs are four kinds of waste:**
>
> | Kind | Activities |
> |---|---|
> | **Logistics waste** | **Storing** · **Moving** |
> | **Control waste** | **Checks & controls** · **Inspection** |
> | **Failure waste** | **Reworking** · **Reconciliation** |
> | **Urgency waste** | **Expediting** |
>
> **Store, Move, Check, Inspect, Fix, Reconcile, Rush.**
>
> Reading them this way explains *why* each is non-value-adding. **The customer pays for the product, not for it being moved, counted, checked, corrected or hurried.** Every one of these exists because of a defect elsewhere — you inspect because you do not trust the previous step, you reconcile because two systems disagree, you expedite because the schedule slipped.

> [!TRAP]
> The deck attaches an important caution to the NVA classification:
>
> > **"Word of caution on non-value activities. Even though most of these do not add value, they may have some value such as management information. One needs to separate this and retain / transfer this in some other step."**
>
> **Non-value-adding is not the same as useless.** An inspection step may be the only place a quality metric is captured; a reconciliation may be a statutory requirement. Delete it blindly and you lose the information or breach the regulation.
>
> The instruction is to **retain or transfer the useful part into another step** — not simply to cut. This is the same judgement Unit 1 required of gap analysis: understand *why* something exists before removing it.

## Common process problems

> [!EXAM]
> **Some common process problems:**
> - **Clear ownership of process missing** — **no end-to-end ownership**
> - **Too many hand-offs**
> - **Wrong measurements** — in terms of **task performance rather than process performance**
> - **Sequential execution of steps**
> - **A large number of audits and reconciliations which do not add value for the customer**
> - **Very few of the process steps are value adding**

> [!EXAM]
> **Reasons for process problems:**
> - **Several hand-offs and transfers of ownership in the processes**
> - **Fragmentation of process across several departments**
> - **Process is underdesigned or overdesigned**
> - **Poor training and education of process participants**

> [!DERIVE]
> **The deck also gives four historical reasons why processes end up this way** — and these are the answer to *"why did nobody fix this earlier?"*:
>
> 1. **Non-availability of technology in the beginning** — the process was designed around a constraint that no longer exists
> 2. **Process copied from another company / parent organization** — it was never designed for *this* business
> 3. **Process brought in as a temporary measure to fix some problem, but never reviewed for permanent effect**
> 4. **Process there to suit some set of individuals**
>
> Reason 3 is the most common and the most human: a workaround becomes permanent because nobody revisits it once the crisis passes. Reason 4 is the most political, and the hardest to change.
>
> The deck's note is the sharp part: **"Many of these are problems because one is looking at it. Else they continue to be the way of working!"** Nobody experiences these as problems day to day — they are simply *how things are done here*. **BPR's first contribution is making them visible.**

> [!TRAP]
> **"Wrong measurements — task performance rather than process performance"** is the diagnosis-phase version of Unit 1's *Production → Quantity, Sales → Variety* conflict.
>
> If every department is measured on its own task, every department optimises its own task, and **nobody is measured on the outcome the customer experiences.** Note that fixing this is not a process change at all — it is an **organizational design** change, which is why *"measurements and incentives at process level rather than individual functional"* appears as one of the **levers of process redesign**.

> [!INTUITION]
> **"Sequential execution of steps"** is listed as a *problem*, which surprises people — surely steps happen in order?
>
> Not necessarily. Many steps are sequential only because a **paper document** could physically be in one place at a time. Once information is in a shared database, credit checking and inventory checking can run **in parallel**. That is why *"parallelize work"* is a redesign principle and why **IT is BPR's primary enabler** — it removes the constraint that made sequence necessary.
