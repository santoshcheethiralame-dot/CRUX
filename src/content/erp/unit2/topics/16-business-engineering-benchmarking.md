# Business Engineering, Benchmarking & Best Practices

## Business Engineering

> [!EXAM]
> **Business Engineering (BE) = Business Process Reengineering (BPR) + Information Technology (IT)**
>
> $$\textbf{BE} = \textbf{BPR} + \textbf{IT}$$
>
> **IT changes the rules of business — Disruptive:**
> - **Data available anywhere to anybody with control**
> - **IT / software systems enable even a non-expert to do an expert job**
> - **Decision support tools for everybody**
> - **Plans get revised instantaneously**

> [!TRAP]
> The deck adds a caveat that is worth stating in any answer on this:
>
> > **"BPR is possible without IT also! Though for our discussion, BPR and BE we are treating as equivalent in the context of ERP implementation."**
>
> **BPR is not inherently a technology activity.** You could reengineer a process using only paper, people and better rules. What IT does is **enlarge the space of possible designs** — which is why the BPR-vs-TQM table lists **IT as BPR's primary enabler**.
>
> So *BE = BPR + IT* is a **scoping statement for this course**, not a definition of BPR itself.

> [!INTUITION]
> The four bullets on how IT is disruptive each **remove a constraint** that shaped old processes:
>
> | IT capability | Constraint it removes | Redesign it enables |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Data available anywhere** | information lived in one filing cabinet | **parallelize work** · **no handoff** |
> | **Non-expert can do an expert job** | only a specialist could handle the case | **case manager as SPOC** · **combine jobs into one** |
> | **Decision support for everybody** | decisions had to escalate to who had the data | **workers make decisions** · **reduce audits** |
> | **Plans revised instantaneously** | replanning took a cycle, so plans were rigid | **flexibility to change** |
>
> **Every redesign principle from the previous topic depends on one of these.** That is the real content of *BE = BPR + IT*: IT does not automate the old process, it makes a **different** process possible.
>
> Read the second row against the case-manager example: one person replaced financing, credit checking, approval and issuance **because the system supplies the expertise** each of those roles used to hold.

## BPR and ERP — the middle path

> [!EXAM]
> **Risks of doing a full-blown BPR before ERP implementation:**
> - **It takes a long time and cost to implement**
> - **There is no guarantee that those best-designed processes will be supported by the ERP solution**
> - **This whole approach may take three to four years, and by then the company's business dynamics can change** with new acquisitions, etc.
>
> **Most favour the middle path:**
> - **Follow the best practice processes that come along with the ERP solution**
> - **Do a limited BPR of existing processes (not a full-blown BPR)** in a way that **gives them competitive advantage**, ensuring the process is supported by the ERP solution

> [!DERIVE]
> **The second risk is the decisive one.** You spend three years designing an ideal process, then discover the ERP **cannot support it** — so every difference becomes a **gap**, and every gap must be closed by **customization**, which Unit 1 identified as the central risk of Realization.
>
> **Full-blown BPR before ERP manufactures the exact problem gap analysis is meant to minimise.**
>
> The deck's notes add two more objections: *"change management can be difficult, impact assessment could be difficult, can confuse people"* — because you are asking the organisation to absorb a redesigned process **and** a new system, which is the *dosage* failure from Unit 1's *"many implementations driven purely by BPR have failed."*

> [!EXAM]
> **The counter-risk of relying on ERP best practices:**
>
> > **"Strong bias towards ERP driven best practice is quite natural — may lead to losing competitive advantage. So BPR team / implementers need to look for differentiators. Do not lose out on the company's special competence."**

> [!INTUITION]
> This is the **homogenization paradox** resolved into a working rule:
>
> - For processes where you have **no competitive advantage** — payroll, accounts payable, general ledger — **take the vendor's best practice**. There is nothing to be gained by being different.
> - For the few processes that **are** your advantage — Wal-Mart's replenishment, Toyota's production system — **do the limited BPR** and make the ERP support it.
>
> **Standardise where you don't compete; differentiate where you do.** That single sentence answers most exam questions about the relationship between BPR and ERP.

## Benchmarking

> [!EXAM]
> **Definition:**
>
> > **"The process of comparing one's business processes and performance metrics to industry bests and/or best practices from other industries."**

> [!EXAM]
> **The five types of benchmarking:**
>
> | Type | Focus |
> |---|---|
> | **Process benchmarking** | **Business processes** — identifying and observing best practices from one or more benchmark companies |
> | **Financial benchmarking** | **Financial analysis** of the company and its benchmark to assess **overall competitiveness and productivity, in terms of money** |
> | **Competitiveness benchmarking** | **Assessing competitive position** by comparing **products and services** with those of target firms |
> | **Product benchmarking** | **Designing new products or upgrades** to current ones |
> | **Functional benchmarking** | Focus on **a single function** (for example, Human Resources) to improve that particular function |

> [!EXAM]
> **Benchmarking — Steps:**
> 1. **Identify focus area**
> 2. **Identify leaders / competition / standards**
> 3. **Identify measures / best practices**
> 4. **Align & implement suitable practices**
>
> **The named exemplars:**
> - **Toyota — Just-in-Time (JIT) inventory**
> - **Motorola — Six Sigma quality management**
> - **Wal-Mart — Vendor Managed Inventory (VMI)**

> [!TRAP]
> The deck is candid about benchmarking's weaknesses, and quoting this earns credit:
>
> > **"Any benchmarking has many known 'games' and pitfalls — comparing 'apples' to 'oranges'."**
>
> Two companies' "order fulfilment time" may measure different things, start at different points, or exclude different exceptions. A benchmark that is not **like-for-like** produces a target that is meaningless or unreachable.
>
> The deck also notes the *purposes* are not all analytical: benchmarking is done **"to improve, to sell — brand, to size the environment."** Some benchmarking exists to be published, not to be learned from — which is why the BPR headline numbers were flagged as *"true and false simultaneously."*

> [!INTUITION]
> **When no external yardstick exists**, the deck notes: *"one tends to compare to oneself at a different period — as seen in most annual reports of companies."*
>
> **Self-benchmarking over time** is weaker than external comparison — it can only tell you whether you improved, never whether you are competitive. But it is honest about like-for-like, since the definitions are your own.

> [!EXAM]
> **Relevance of benchmarking in ERP projects — "competitive advantage is the key."** The deck's note is emphatic:
>
> > **"ERP projects MUST carefully look at benchmark results. An ERP project should not ruin the current advantages. Must plan for long term competitive advantage and make room for processes that can bring advantages."**
>
> Note also that **benchmarking is one of the three decision factors for process selection** — it is how you find which processes are worth reengineering.

## Best practices

> [!EXAM]
> **Best practices are one of the reasons to bring in ERP.** How do they come into ERP? **In the form of:**
> - **Specific processes**
> - **Pre-configured settings**
> - **Business blueprints**
> - **Test process & data**
> - **Master data**

> [!EXAM]
> **Where the best practices come from:**
> - **From leading corporations** — **Kanban / JIT by Toyota** in particular; **VMI — vendor managed inventory of Wal-Mart**
> - **Industry group initiatives**
> - **ERP vendors' experience**
> - **Industry / geography specific**
>
> **SAP's best practice offer:** **industry specific** (such as for retail) · **cross industry** · **baseline package**.

> [!INTUITION]
> **"Business blueprints" appearing in that list is worth noticing.** Unit 1 made Business Blueprinting a whole phase of the life cycle — modelling As-Is, designing To-Be, gap analysis.
>
> A vendor shipping **pre-built blueprints** means part of that phase arrives already done. That is a genuine accelerator, and it is why **ASAP's objectives include "quick and cost effective implementation"** and **"re-usability."**
>
> It is also where the **homogenization** risk enters most quietly: adopt the shipped blueprint and you have adopted someone else's process design without ever consciously deciding to.

> [!TRAP]
> The deck's closing caution on best practices:
>
> > **"Of course, many times vendors force fit some of the not-so-great features also as best practices."**
>
> And: **"It is a little tricky to 'pinpoint' best practice."**
>
> A vendor calling something a best practice is partly a **marketing claim**. Some shipped processes are genuinely distilled from leading corporations; others are simply how that product happens to work. **The BPR team's job is to tell the difference** — which is exactly why benchmarking exists as an independent yardstick.
