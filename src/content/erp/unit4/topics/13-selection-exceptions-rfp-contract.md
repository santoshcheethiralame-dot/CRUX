# Selection Exceptions, RFP & Vendor Contract

## When the process is not followed

> [!EXAM]
> **"Lots of companies do not follow the traditional process for ERP package selection", for reasons like:**
>
> 1. **Pressure from major customers to go for a particular ERP so that it can be on the same platform as their customer**
> 2. **Same ERP for all the companies in the same group, to solve problems of integration / business consolidation etc.**
> 3. **Price becomes the most dominant factor for ERP selection (especially for SMB)**
> 4. **Industry specific functionality**
>
> The question bank asks this as: *"Give some examples and reasons why in some cases the package selection is not done in the traditional way and gets influenced by other factors."*

> [!INTUITION]
> **These are not four failures of discipline — three of them are perfectly rational, and saying so is the mature answer.**
>
> | Reason | Why it is rational |
> |---|---|
> | **Customer pressure** | if your largest customer requires EDI on their platform, **integration with them may be worth more than a better internal fit** |
> | **Group standardisation** | one ERP across a group makes **consolidation, shared services and inter-company transfers** work — a group-level benefit no single-company evaluation would ever measure |
> | **Price dominant for SMB** | a small company may simply **not be able to afford** the best-fit package; the real choice is *the affordable one or nothing* |
> | **Industry-specific functionality** | if only one package does **oil-field exploration** or **clinical trials**, the evaluation is already over |
>
> **The common structure: a constraint outside the evaluation outweighs everything inside it.**
>
> **Reason 2 is the direct answer to Unit 1's Nestlé problem** — nine general ledgers, twenty-nine vanilla records — which arose precisely because divisions chose independently. **Standardising the package is how a group stops that happening again**, and it is worth accepting a worse local fit to get it.

> [!TRAP]
> **Reason 4 sits awkwardly with the others and is the one to be careful about.**
>
> *Customer pressure*, *group standardisation* and *price* genuinely bypass the criteria. But **industry-specific functionality *is* one of the criteria** — it is **Industry Experience** on the selection diagram, and the industry-specific installed base is named under **Installed Base**.
>
> **So reason 4 is better described as one criterion dominating all the others** than as skipping the process. When a package is the only one supporting your industry's core process, **functionality fit scores so overwhelmingly that the weighted comparison has only one possible outcome** — which is exactly why the deck's own examples are **Mincom in Mining, Retek for Retail, Ramco for Utilities.**

## The RFP process

> [!EXAM]
> - **Some of the large organizations follow the RFP (Request For Proposal) process for selecting an ERP package. It is common for large ERP projects**
> - **RFP process is a must for Public Sector Organizations**
>
> **A typical RFP document can contain details like:**
> - **High Level Scope and Project Duration**
> - **Overview of the Company's business and operations**
> - **Business benefits the company is expecting from the project**
> - **Proposal submission details — when and how the offer is to be submitted**
> - **Payment schedule, Bonus, Penalty etc.**

> [!DERIVE]
> **The five sections are not arbitrary — each exists so that competing bids are actually comparable.**
>
> | Section | Without it… |
> |---|---|
> | **Scope and duration** | vendors bid on **different amounts of work**, so the prices mean nothing |
> | **Overview of business and operations** | vendors cannot size the effort — how many plants, users, countries? |
> | **Expected business benefits** | vendors cannot propose **how** to deliver value, only what to install |
> | **Submission details** | bids arrive in different formats at different times — **no fair comparison, no audit trail** |
> | **Payment schedule, bonus, penalty** | commercial terms are **negotiated separately per vendor**, and the cheapest bid may have the worst terms |
>
> **An RFP is a device for forcing a single question on every bidder so the answers can be laid side by side.** That is why it is *"a must for Public Sector Organizations"*: a public body must be able to **prove** afterwards that the process was fair, and identical questions with dated, documented responses are that proof.
>
> **Note that "bonus" appears alongside "penalty."** Penalties for late delivery are expected; **a bonus for early or superior delivery is the less obvious half**, and it exists because a purely punitive contract makes the vendor defensive and conservative rather than committed.

## Contracting with the package vendor

> [!EXAM]
> **Salient points of a contract with an ERP package vendor:**
>
> 1. **Payment terms**
> 2. **Intellectual property or copyright clauses**
> 3. **List of deliverables along with software**
> 4. **How training in the software will be handled**
> 5. **How the software maintenance will be handled**
> 6. **How future upgrades in the software will be provided and at what cost**
> 7. **Software installation help to be provided**
> 8. **Prerequisites to install the software** — **the hardware specification, operating system needed, database needed etc.**
> 9. **Any audit or review by the package vendor during package implementation** *(like **Final blueprint audit**, **Audit before go live**)*
> 10. **License cancellation costs**
>
> **And the framing statement, which is the reason the list is this long:**
>
> > *"**ERP package procurement is not a one-time transaction with the ERP vendor. Help of the ERP vendor is needed throughout the package life cycle — for supporting the application, for upgrading, for training, etc.**"*

> [!INTUITION]
> **Sort the ten clauses by *when* they bite and the contract's logic appears:**
>
> | When | Clauses |
> |---|---|
> | **At purchase** | payment terms · list of deliverables · IP / copyright |
> | **During implementation** | installation help · prerequisites · training · **vendor audits** |
> | **For years afterwards** | maintenance · **upgrades and their cost** |
> | **At the end** | **licence cancellation costs** |
>
> **Only three of the ten concern the purchase itself.** Six govern a relationship that outlives the project, and one governs the exit. **That distribution is the TCO argument written as a legal document.**

> [!TRAP]
> **Three clauses are the ones that hurt if you omit them, and they are the ones worth naming in an answer.**
>
> **① "How future upgrades will be provided *and at what cost*."** Without a cost commitment, you are locked in with no price protection: the vendor knows your switching cost, and every mandatory upgrade becomes a negotiation you cannot walk away from.
>
> **② "License cancellation costs."** You negotiate the exit **while you still have leverage** — i.e. before signing, while they still want the deal. Afterwards you have none. **This is the single clause most often left out and most expensive to lack.**
>
> **③ "Any audit or review by the package vendor during implementation."** This one is *not* a customer protection and reads oddly in a list of things you want. The named examples — **final blueprint audit** and **audit before go live** — are the **vendor** checking **your** implementation.
>
> Understand why it belongs there: **the vendor's product gets blamed when an implementation fails**, so the vendor has a genuine interest in catching a bad configuration before go-live. **You want that check, and you want it agreed in advance** rather than discovering mid-project that the vendor expects to audit you — or, worse, that they will not, and nobody independent is looking at your blueprint at all.
>
> Note where those two audits fall: **blueprint** and **before go-live** are exactly the ends of the **Realization** phase, and the go-live audit reappears in full detail in the project-management chapter.

> [!EXAM]
> **Compare the two RFPs in this unit** — the deck reuses almost the same five bullets for **package selection** and for **consulting partner selection**, with one addition on the partner side: **an RFI (Request For Information) is sometimes issued prior to the RFP**, and *"based on RFI response a few vendors can be shortlisted among many."*
>
> **RFI shortlists; RFP selects.** It is the same two-step *screening then selection* structure as package selection, in procurement clothing.
