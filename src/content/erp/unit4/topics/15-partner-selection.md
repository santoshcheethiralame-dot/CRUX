# ERP Consulting Partner Selection

## Why it matters, and why there may be several

> [!EXAM]
> - **Selecting the right consulting partner is crucial for ERP project success**
> - **There can be different consulting partners for different phases of the project like:**
>   - **one for package selection**
>   - **a second one for doing package implementation**
>   - **a third one for doing all technical works from a low cost country**
> - **Large global implementations these days are mostly broken between multiple partners**
> - **When multiple partners are involved in an implementation, coordination between different players becomes extremely important**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Why a company would deliberately hire three firms instead of one** is worth understanding, because it looks like needless complexity.
>
> | Partner | Why a *different* firm |
> |---|---|
> | **Package selection** | a firm that also sells implementation services has an **interest in which package you pick** — the one they staff best. **Separating selection from implementation removes that conflict.** |
> | **Implementation** | this is the bulk of the work and needs deep **product** expertise |
> | **Offshore technical work** | development and testing are **cost-sensitive and location-independent** — pay onsite rates for them and you overpay |
>
> **Conflict of interest, expertise, and cost — three different reasons, three different firms.**
>
> **And the deck immediately names the price of doing it: *"coordination between different players becomes extremely important."*** Every boundary between firms is a boundary where work can fall through, where blame can be deflected, and where nobody owns the outcome end to end.
>
> **That is exactly why "Ability to Provide End-to-End Service" is one of the ten selection criteria** — it is the alternative to multi-sourcing, bought deliberately.

## The selection effort

> [!EXAM]
> - **Size of the selection team: 4–10 people, could be 60 for large organizations**
> - **Time: two to six months, up to one year is not unusual**
> - **Reports from consultancy firms**
> - **Follow selection process**

> [!TRAP]
> **"Two to six months, up to one year is not unusual" — for choosing the implementer, before any implementation begins.** Add the package selection alongside it and **the selecting can take as long as a small implementation.**
>
> That is not waste, and the reason connects to the risk chapter: **"poor ERP package selection"** and **"inadequate selection"** are both named causes of ERP failure, and the mitigation slide lists exactly four remedies — **appropriate selection team, right selection process, adequate budget, adequate time allocated.**
>
> **Two of the four mitigations are the team and the time this slide specifies.** The months spent selecting are risk mitigation with a price tag, and the deck is pricing them.

## Two ways of selecting a vendor

> [!EXAM]
> **Ways of selecting a vendor:**
>
> - **Competitive bidding:** **the vendors bid and the most competitive one is awarded the contract, provided he fulfills the requirements**
> - **Two-phase acquisition:** **the selected bidders are asked to do some additional work, on which they are evaluated and awarded the contract**
>
> **ERP vendors are generally selected through competitive bidding only.**

> [!DERIVE]
> **The difference is what the decision is based on: a *promise* or a *performance*.**
>
> | | **Competitive bidding** | **Two-phase acquisition** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Evaluated on | **the proposal** — what they say they will do and at what price | **actual work delivered** in a paid or unpaid second phase |
> | Evidence quality | **claims**, backed by references | **demonstrated capability** |
> | Cost and time | **lower** | **higher** — you are paying several firms to do real work |
>
> **Two-phase acquisition buys better evidence at a higher price.** The extra phase — a pilot, a proof of concept, a design study — shows you how the firm actually works before you commit the full contract.
>
> **Note the qualifier in the competitive-bidding definition: *"provided he fulfills the requirements."*** It is not simply the lowest bid. Bids clear a technical threshold first and compete on price second — which is exactly the **technical and financial** two-stage bid evaluation named in the RFP slide.
>
> **And the deck's verdict — "ERP vendors are generally selected through competitive bidding only" — is a fact about practice, not a recommendation.** Two-phase would give better evidence; it is rarely used because few firms will do substantial unpaid work, and paying several to do it is expensive.

## The ten selection criteria

> [!EXAM]
> **Selection criteria for an ERP consulting partner:**
>
> 1. **Vendor's Industry Experience**
> 2. **Process Consulting Experience**
> 3. **Client Reference and Client Satisfaction Level**
> 4. **Ability to Provide End-to-End Service**
> 5. **Quality of Project Management**
> 6. **Quality of Tools and Methodologies**
> 7. **Presence in Different Geographies / Global Presence**
> 8. **Global Delivery Capability**
> 9. **Reputation and Branding**
> 10. **Price and Value for Money**
>
> **Selection matrix** for partner comparative evaluation and selection.

> [!INTUITION]
> **Group them by what is being assessed:**
>
> | Group | Criteria |
> |---|---|
> | **What do they know?** | **Industry Experience · Process Consulting Experience** |
> | **How do they work?** | **Quality of Project Management · Quality of Tools and Methodologies · End-to-End Service** |
> | **Can they reach us?** | **Presence in Different Geographies · Global Delivery Capability** |
> | **What do others say, and what does it cost?** | **Client Reference & Satisfaction · Reputation and Branding · Price and Value for Money** |
>
> **Know · work · reach · reputation.**

> [!TRAP]
> **Compare this list against the package criteria and the shape of the difference is examinable.**
>
> | | **Package criteria** | **Partner criteria** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Dominant concern | **will the vendor survive?** — financial viability, product strategy, installed base | **can these people actually deliver?** — experience, methodology, project management |
> | Absent from the other list | financial viability, technology maturity, TCO | **process consulting experience, quality of project management, global delivery** |
>
> **You buy a package for a decade, so you assess the vendor as an institution. You buy a partner for a project, so you assess their capability to execute.**
>
> **"Price and Value for Money" is deliberately not just "Price"** — the cheapest bid on a project where **"poor project team skills"** and **"ineffective vendor and consultant"** are named failure causes is a false economy.
>
> ⚠️ **Two criteria are easy to conflate: "Presence in Different Geographies" and "Global Delivery Capability."** *Presence* means they have offices and people **where your sites are** — someone can be on the ground in Chennai and in Frankfurt. *Global delivery capability* means they can **run work across locations**, moving it offshore and coordinating time zones. **Being in many places is not the same as being able to work across them.**
>
> **Note also what is missing: product/package expertise is not on the list at all** — presumably assumed. The in-house-vs-external slide, however, names it as the very first advantage an external team has: **"Product Expertise."**

## The RFP method

> [!EXAM]
> - **Some large organizations follow the RFP (Request For Proposal) process for selecting a consulting partner for ERP implementation**
> - **RFP process is a must for Public Sector Organizations**
> - **Sometimes an RFI (Request For Information) is issued prior to RFP.** **Based on RFI response a few vendors can be shortlisted among many**
>
> **An RFP can contain:** **High level scope and project duration · Overview of the company's business and operations · Business benefits the company is expecting from the project · Proposal submission details — when and how the offer is to be submitted · Payment schedule, Bonus, Penalty, etc.**
>
> **Sections of a typical RFP:** **Scope of project · Duration of project · Payment terms with schedule · Performance bonus / penalty clause**
>
> **Bids are evaluated on two dimensions: Technical and Financial.**

> [!EXAM]
> **RFP process steps, in order:**
>
> 1. **RFI Process Outputs** *(optional)*
> 2. **RFP Preparation**
> 3. **RFP Release — to selected vendors** · **Conference Call** · **Common Meeting** · **Media Advertisement**
> 4. **RFP Response Collection**
> 5. **RFP Evaluation**
> 6. **Partner Selection**
> 7. **Contract Negotiations**
> 8. **Partner Finalization**
> 9. **Contract Signing**
> 10. **Project Implementation Start / Kick off**

> [!DERIVE]
> **Three details in that sequence are worth noticing, because they are where the marks are.**
>
> **① The RFI is optional and comes first.** It is a cheap, low-effort request for basic information used to **cut a long list down to a shortlist**, before anyone invests in writing a full proposal. **RFI shortlists; RFP selects** — the same two-step screening-then-selection economics as package selection, and the same reason: *spend the expensive evaluation only on survivors.*
>
> **② Release is not just "send the document."** The deck lists **conference call · common meeting · media advertisement** alongside it. The first two exist so that **every bidder hears the same clarifications at the same time** — answering one vendor's question privately would advantage them. The third, **media advertisement**, is what makes the process **open** rather than invitation-only, which is again why it is mandatory in the public sector.
>
> **③ Selection and finalization are separate steps with negotiation between them.**
>
> $$\textbf{Partner Selection} \rightarrow \textbf{Contract Negotiations} \rightarrow \textbf{Partner Finalization} \rightarrow \textbf{Contract Signing}$$
>
> **You are selected-but-not-final while terms are negotiated** — and that gap is deliberate leverage. If negotiations fail, the company can still turn to the runner-up. **This is exactly why the package shortlist is kept at "two to a maximum of three" rather than one.**
>
> **And "technical and financial" bid evaluation is the same two-threshold structure as competitive bidding's *"provided he fulfills the requirements"***: clear the technical bar first, then compete on price. **A cheap bid that fails technically is not a bid.**
