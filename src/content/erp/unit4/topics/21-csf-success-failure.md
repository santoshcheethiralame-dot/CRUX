# CSFs, Success & Failure of ERP Implementations

## What a CSF is

> [!EXAM]
> **Critical Success Factors (CSF):**
> - **These are the factors vital for the success of an ERP project. If addressed, CSFs improve the chances for success of an ERP project significantly**
> - **CSFs have been divided into strategic and tactical factors.** The groups are further divided into **strategic, tactical, operational, organizational and technological**
> - **CSFs can also be looked at from the perspective of stakeholders**

> [!EXAM]
> **Categories of Critical Success Factors:**
>
> - **Careful selection of ERP package and implementation partner**
> - **Clear goals and objectives**
> - **Project management**
> - **Interdepartmental communication and co-operation**
> - **Training and change management**
> - **Project team competence**
> - **Top management support**
> - **Vendor support**
> - **Organizational characteristics**
> - **ERP system**
> - **User involvement**
> - **Project monitoring**
> - **Environment**
> - **Implementation strategy**
> - **Software development**

> [!INTUITION]
> **A CSF is not a task — it is a condition.** The definition says *"if addressed, CSFs improve the chances for success significantly"*, which is a probabilistic claim, not a guarantee. **You cannot tick a CSF off; you either have it or you do not, throughout.**
>
> **Group the categories and four clusters appear:**
>
> | Cluster | CSFs |
> |---|---|
> | **Decisions made before the project** | careful selection of package and partner · clear goals and objectives · implementation strategy |
> | **How the project is run** | project management · project monitoring · project team competence · software development |
> | **The organisation around it** | top management support · user involvement · interdepartmental communication · training and change management · organizational characteristics |
> | **What you are working with** | ERP system · vendor support · environment |
>
> **The largest cluster is the organisation, not the project.** That is the single most repeated finding of this entire course, and it is why Unit 2 exists.

## Reasons implementations succeed

> [!EXAM]
> **Reasons Why ERP Implementations Succeed:**
>
> - **ERP package selection needs to be well thought through, understanding the company's business requirements and long term goals**
> - **Expectations from the ERP system need to be realistic and to be documented**
> - **Investment in ERP needs to have proper ROI justification**
> - **ERP systems are not implemented to cater to today's business requirements only, but also to cater to the business needs of the future**
> - **Support of top management is of utmost importance**
> - **Company's best brains need to work along with the consultants to design tomorrow's business processes and information systems**
> - **User acceptance is critical — without that, even if the system goes LIVE, it will never be used by end users**
> - **Handling employee resistance is critical for implementation to succeed**
> - **Training people at every level**
> - **Communication is essential for success of ERP**
> - **ERP project should not be viewed as an IT project but as a business project that involves business people**
> - **For an ERP project to succeed it is also important to minimize developments and customization in the software and, to the extent possible, use the standard software**
> - **The project plan and duration of the project need to be well planned, providing adequate time for each activity**
> - **For projects to succeed the core team needs to be full time**

> [!DERIVE]
> **The success list and the failure list are the same list with the signs flipped**, and demonstrating that is a strong exam answer.
>
> | Success factor | Its failure twin |
> |---|---|
> | well-thought package selection | **poor ERP package selection** |
> | realistic, documented expectations | **inadequate requirement definition and unrealistic expectations** |
> | proper ROI justification | **investment without ROI justification** |
> | caters to future needs | **not able to support changing business needs** |
> | top management support | **top management support lacking** |
> | best brains with the consultants | **ERP core team do not have the right resource** |
> | user acceptance | **user acceptance issues** |
> | handling employee resistance | **employees resist ERP implementation** |
> | training at every level | **training issues for the team** |
> | communication | **poor communication** |
> | business project, not IT project | **project viewed as an IT project** |
> | minimize customization | **customizing the software too much** |
> | adequate time per activity | **too tight project schedule** |
> | full-time core team | **high turnover rate of project team members** |
>
> **Fourteen pairs, one-to-one.** That symmetry is not padding — it means **there is no separate theory of failure**: projects fail by the absence of the things that make them succeed.
>
> **The most quotable single line is on user acceptance:** ***"without that, even if the system goes LIVE, it will never be used by end users."*** **Go-live is not success.** A system that is technically live and practically bypassed — people keeping their spreadsheets, entering data late or not at all — has failed while appearing to have succeeded.

## Things to take care of

> [!EXAM]
> **Things to be taken care of for a successful ERP implementation:**
>
> 1. **How you define success — define measures**
> 2. **Managing project scope**
> 3. **Requirements prioritization**
> 4. **Have early success and pilots**
> 5. **Have a good relationship with the ERP vendor and consulting partner**
> 6. **Good project management**
> 7. **Having clean data in the system**
> 8. **Quick decisions**
> 9. **Test the solution thoroughly**
> 10. **Ensure that the project has sufficient budget till the end**
> 11. **Efficient risk management**
> 12. **Process owner's involvement**
> 13. **Who owns business benefits**

> [!TRAP]
> **Four of these are easy to skim past and are the most valuable.**
>
> **① *"How you define success — define measures."*** This is first on the list for a reason. Unit 2's Gartner finding was that **70% of failed ERP projects did not use performance metrics** and **27% did not measure at all.** **A project with no definition of success cannot succeed** — it can only stop.
>
> **② *"Have early success and pilots."*** Momentum is a real resource. An early visible win **buys credibility with sceptical users and with the steering committee funding you**, and it is the same argument as the **phased approach** in risk mitigation.
>
> **③ *"Quick decisions."*** An unglamorous item that matters enormously. ERP projects are **decision-dense** — hundreds of design choices, each blocking configuration until resolved. **A decision that takes three weeks instead of three days idles a team**, and *"organizational politics"* is on the risk diagram largely because of what it does to decision speed.
>
> **④ *"Who owns business benefits."*** The sharpest item on the list. The project delivers a **system**; the **benefits** — lower inventory, faster order cycle — are realised by the business afterwards, long after the project team disbands. **If no named person owns them, nobody will pursue them**, and the ROI justification quietly becomes fiction. This is why the **Process Owner** role exists and is drawn *from the business side*.
>
> **And ⑩ — *"sufficient budget till the end"* — pairs with the failure list's "costs getting out of hand", which is flagged there as *"a symptom, not a cause."*** Running out of money at 90% is the worst possible outcome: you have paid for everything and got nothing.

## Reasons implementations fail

> [!EXAM]
> **Reasons Why ERP Implementations Fail:**
>
> **Poor ERP package selection · Inadequate requirement definition and unrealistic expectations, scope · Investment without ROI justification · Not able to support changing business needs · Top management support lacking · ERP core team do not have the right resource · User acceptance issues · Employees resist ERP implementation · Training issues for the team · Poor communication · Project viewed as an IT project · Customizing the software too much · Too tight project schedule, miscalculation of time and effort · High turnover rate of project team members**

> [!EXAM]
> **Most cited reasons for ERP implementation project failure — the deck's ranked table:**
>
> | Reason | % |
> |---|---|
> | **Poor communication between relevant parties** | **57%** |
> | **Lack of planning of scheduling, resources and activities** | **39%** |
> | **No quality control** | **34%** |
> | **Milestones not being set** | **29%** |
> | **Inadequate co-ordination of resources** | **26%** |
> | **Costs getting out of hand** *(it is a symptom not a cause)* | **20%** |
> | **Mismanagement of progress** *(overlaps with poor communication)* | **17%** |
> | **Overall poor management** | **13%** |
> | **Suppliers skills overstretched** | **12%** |
> | **Supplier under-resourced** | **11%** |

> [!DERIVE]
> **This table is the most examinable single artefact in the chapter, and three things about it deserve comment.**
>
> **① Communication leads at 57% — nearly half again the second place.** And the deck itself notes that **"mismanagement of progress" (17%) overlaps with poor communication**, so the true figure is higher still. **The number one cause of ERP failure is not technical.**
>
> **② The deck flags "costs getting out of hand" as *a symptom, not a cause*.** That is a genuinely sophisticated annotation and worth repeating in an answer. Costs do not rise spontaneously — **they rise because scope changed, or the schedule slipped, or rework was needed.** Treating the cost overrun as the problem leads to the wrong remedy: cutting testing and training, which makes the actual causes worse.
>
> **③ The top five are all project-management failures**, not ERP-specific ones: communication, planning, quality control, milestones, coordination. **They are exactly the "risk associated with every project" tier of the taxonomy.**
>
> $$\underbrace{\text{57\% + 39\% + 34\% + 29\% + 26\%}}_{\textbf{generic project management}} \;\gg\; \underbrace{\text{12\% + 11\%}}_{\textbf{supplier problems}}$$
>
> **ERP projects mostly fail for the reasons all large projects fail** — which is precisely why the PMBOK areas and the plan/standards/charter apparatus are in this chapter at all.

## Real failed implementations

> [!EXAM]
> **Some failed ERP implementations, as the deck records them:**
>
> | Company | Package / Partner | What happened |
> |---|---|---|
> | **W. L. Gore and Associates** | **PeopleSoft Inc.**, implemented by **Deloitte & Touche** | **October 1997: system went live; faulty. Deloitte & Touche not experts** |
> | **FoxMeyer Drug Co.** | **SAP America**, implemented by **Anderson Consulting** | **Bankruptcy, 1998. Could not handle the huge volume of transactions and had integration problems** |
> | **Cleveland State University** | **PeopleSoft**, implemented by **Kaludis Consulting Group** | **Full of bugs; extra hardware** |
> | **Hershey** | **SAP AG R/3 in 1999**, implemented by **IBM** | **Bugs** |

> [!TRAP]
> **Every one of these four names a Tier 1 package and a Tier 1 or 2 consulting firm** — SAP, PeopleSoft, Deloitte, Anderson, IBM. **Choosing famous names is not a defence.**
>
> **Map each case to the framework and the pattern is clear:**
>
> | Case | Failure mode | Framework item |
> |---|---|---|
> | **W. L. Gore** | *"Deloitte & Touche **not experts**"* | **partner selection** — *vendor's industry experience*, *process consulting experience* |
> | **FoxMeyer** | *"could not handle the **huge volume of transactions**"* | **package selection / functionality fit** — and **testing**: *volume testing* is on the offshoring list for a reason |
> | **Cleveland State** | *"full of bugs; **extra hardware**"* | **testing** and **technology scope** — hardware prerequisites are a named contract clause |
> | **Hershey** | *"bugs"*, **1999** | **testing** and **cutover** |
>
> **FoxMeyer is the case worth knowing in detail** because the outcome was **bankruptcy** — the ERP project did not merely fail, it took the company with it. A drug distributor's entire business *is* high-volume transaction processing; a system that cannot process the volume is not a degraded system, it is **no system**. In the impact vocabulary of the risk chapter, that is **catastrophic — failure of primary mission.**
>
> **Hershey's 1999 go-live is the standard cautionary tale about cutover timing** — it went live approaching its peak season, so the failure hit when the business could least absorb it. Which is exactly why the cutover slide insists on **understanding transaction criticality** and making it **a senior management decision**.
