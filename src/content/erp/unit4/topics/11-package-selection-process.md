# ERP Package Selection — Process & Selection Committee

## Why the choice matters

> [!EXAM]
> - **Selecting the right ERP package that meets a company's business needs is one of the most crucial factors for ERP project success**
> - **Selecting the right ERP solution can be a make or break for the success of the ERP project**
> - **ERP packages come from different origins, different features and at various price points**

> [!EXAM]
> **The deck's four axes of difference between packages, with its own examples:**
>
> | Axis | Examples |
> |---|---|
> | **Strong in a particular process** | **PeopleSoft in Human Resource · BAAN in Manufacturing** |
> | **Strong in a particular industry** | **Mincom in Mining · Retek for Retail · Ramco for Utilities** |
> | **Database support** | **JD Edwards supports DB2 · Oracle supports Oracle database** — while **SAP supports all leading databases** |
> | **Operating system support** | some ERPs **only run on Windows**; others **support all leading OS** |

> [!INTUITION]
> **Those four axes are why "which ERP is best?" has no answer.** A package that is outstanding for a mining company on DB2 may be irrelevant to a retailer on Windows.
>
> Note the pattern in the examples: **the specialists are named by their niche** (Mincom/mining, Retek/retail, Ramco/utilities), while **SAP is named by its breadth** — *"supports all leading databases."*
>
> That is the **Tier 1 vs Tier 3 distinction** appearing before the market slide even arrives: **big players compete on universality, small players compete on fit.** And it is the same *"best-of-breed vs integrated suite"* tension as the inventory-solutions slide.

## The good-fit rule

> [!EXAM]
> - **Several factors need to be considered for this selection**
> - **No package will meet 100% of the company's needs**
> - **Objective is to find a package that is a good-fit (75–80% match) to business requirements and supports long term goals**
>
> **A package that fits well would result in:**
> - **Lower resistance from the users**
> - **Reduced training needs**
> - **Less organizational adaptation issues**
>
> **Gap analysis** is done for figuring out the gap between **the process, data and user interface requirements** and **ERP offerings**.

> [!DERIVE]
> **The 75–80% figure is the most quotable number in this chapter, and the reasoning behind it is examinable.**
>
> **Why not 100%?** Because a package that matched everything would have to be **built for you** — and then it would not be a package. The entire economics of ERP is that **development cost is shared across thousands of customers**; that only works if the software is standard.
>
> **Why not 50%?** Because the missing half must be closed by either **customization** (expensive to build, expensive to maintain, and it blocks upgrades) or **changing your processes** (which is BPR, and which people resist).
>
> $$\textbf{fit} \uparrow \;\Longrightarrow\; \textbf{customization} \downarrow \;\text{ and }\; \textbf{organizational change} \downarrow$$
>
> **The three named benefits are all consequences of that one relationship:**
>
> | Benefit | Because |
> |---|---|
> | **Lower resistance from users** | fewer of their processes have to change |
> | **Reduced training needs** | the system already works the way they do |
> | **Less organizational adaptation issues** | less BPR is forced on the organisation |
>
> And it connects straight to the failure list later in this unit: **"customizing the software too much"** is a named reason ERP implementations fail, and **"minimize developments and customization… use the standard software"** is a named reason they succeed. **A poor-fit package is what forces the customization that then causes the failure.**

> [!TRAP]
> **Gap analysis is defined precisely: the gap between "the process, data and user interface requirements" and "ERP offerings."** Three dimensions, not one — a package can fit the *process* perfectly and still be wrong on the *data model*.
>
> **The deck gives two ways to perform it:**
>
> 1. **Vendor makes a presentation of their ERP system**, or **makes a prototype available to experiment with**. The selection team asks questions, and **based on the answers of the vendor, the gap is established.**
> 2. **The vendor is provided with a detailed requirements document** and **submits a response to all the requirements.**
>
> ⚠️ **Method 1 depends on the vendor's answers; method 2 depends on the vendor's written response.** Both are the vendor grading their own homework — which is exactly why the deck separately insists on **reference sites**, **critical business scenarios run in their system**, and **a demo**. **Independent evidence is the counterweight.**

## Who does the selection

> [!EXAM]
> - **Typically a small team from the company comprising of different department heads and company's top management (CIO or CFO) is part of the selection committee**
> - **Sometimes the selection committee also has a few consultants who know the leading packages well**
> - **This selection committee defines the selection process, the selection criteria, attends all vendors' presentations, puts relevant questions to them, does comparative analysis of different ERP vendors and finally does ERP selection**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Three constituencies, three reasons for being there:**
>
> | Member | Brings |
> |---|---|
> | **Department heads** | *does it do our work?* — the functional requirements |
> | **CIO / CFO** | *can we run it and afford it?* — technical and financial authority |
> | **Consultants** *(sometimes)* | *what are these packages actually like?* — market knowledge nobody inside has |
>
> **The CIO/CFO presence is the load-bearing part.** Selecting an ERP commits the company to a **multi-year, multi-crore, organisation-wide** change. Delegating that to IT alone produces the failure the deck names twice: **"project viewed as an IT project"** rather than a business project.
>
> **And the consultants are there because of an information asymmetry**: the vendors have sold hundreds of these; the company is buying its first. Someone on your side needs to have seen the film before.

## Dealing with ERP vendors

> [!EXAM]
> **Tips for dealing with ERP vendors:**
> - **Have your list of requirements ready and ask vendors how some of these tricky requirements can be met during the presentation**
> - **Ask the vendor to give a demo and to run some of your critical business scenarios in their ERP system**
> - **Ask for reference sites where the selection committee can visit and hear from the users**
> - **Ask the vendor how they differentiate from other ERP vendors**

> [!DERIVE]
> **Each tip is a defence against a specific way of being misled**, and naming which turns four bullets into an argument:
>
> | Tip | Defends against |
> |---|---|
> | **Ask about *tricky* requirements** | a demo tuned to show only what the product does well |
> | **Run *your* critical scenarios** | a generic demo on the vendor's own clean sample data |
> | **Visit *reference sites* and hear from **users**** | the sales team's account of how the last project went |
> | **Ask how they *differentiate*** | every vendor claiming to do everything |
>
> **The common thread: shift the burden of proof from the vendor's words to observable evidence.**
>
> **"Hear from the users" is the sharpest phrase in the list.** Not the CIO who signed the deal and has a reputation invested in it — **the people who use it daily**, who have no reason to defend the decision.

## The two-step process

> [!EXAM]
> **Most companies follow a two-step approach for package selection:**
>
> **Step 1 — Screening**
> - **Evaluation can start with a large number of packages**
> - **Based on a set of criteria, a few of them (say two to a maximum of three) are short listed**
> - **This process is called screening**
>
> **Step 2 — Final selection**
> - **These two or three packages are compared against the list of requirements (100s) of the company**
> - **After the detailed evaluation of these short listed packages, one is selected and the company enters into contract with the selected vendor**

> [!EXAM]
> **The deck's full process sequence:**
>
> $$\textbf{Have ERP selection team in place} \rightarrow \textbf{Decide selection criteria and weights} \rightarrow \textbf{Screen initial list and shortlist a few}$$
> $$\rightarrow \textbf{Detailed final evaluation of shortlisted vendors} \rightarrow \textbf{Finalize the ERP vendor and enter into contract}$$
>
> The **ERP Solution and Vendor Selection** slide expands the same thing:
> - **Gather information about the vendors and their products using available channels and resources**
> - **Preliminary filtering** · **demonstration by the vendors** · **filtering using high-level parameters**
> - **Decide selection parameters** · **prepare and release RFP** · **analyse and select the vendor**
> - **Evaluate vendor's ERP system on certain parameters** · **analysis of financial terms and conditions** · **detailed demonstration by the vendors**

> [!TRAP]
> **Why two steps and not one is the examinable insight**, and the numbers in the deck give it away.
>
> The final evaluation compares packages against **"the list of requirements (100s) of the company."** Running hundreds of requirements against **twenty** packages is thousands of assessments — unaffordable in time and attention.
>
> $$\textbf{screening: many packages} \times \textbf{few criteria} \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad \textbf{selection: few packages} \times \textbf{many criteria}$$
>
> **Cheap criteria eliminate cheaply; expensive criteria are spent only on survivors.** That is the same economics as **Rough Cut Capacity Planning** at MPS level versus **CRP** at MRP level — *check coarsely at the top, finely at the bottom* — and the same as **ABC-driven cycle counting**. **Match the cost of the check to the size of the remaining decision.**
>
> Note the deliberate shortlist size: **"two to a maximum of three."** Small enough that the detailed comparison is affordable; large enough that you retain negotiating leverage right up to contract signature.
