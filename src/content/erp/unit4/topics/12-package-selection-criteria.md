# Package Selection Criteria & Final Evaluation

## The ten criteria

> [!EXAM]
> **Selection criteria for an ERP package**, as the deck's diagram arranges them:
>
> **Functionality Fit · Financial Viability · Consulting Partnerships · Technology Maturity · Product Strategy, Vision, Innovation · Industry Experience · Integration with 3rd Party apps · Package Cost · Customer Service / Support · Local Presence**

> [!EXAM]
> **Criteria for initial package screening**, as the deck's two list slides give them:
>
> 1. **Product Strategy, Vision & Investment in Innovation by Vendor**
> 2. **Financial Viability of the Application Vendor**
> 3. **Installed Base and Local Presence** — **geography / country specific · industry domain specific · MNC**
> 4. **Partnerships** — **implementation partnerships with consulting companies** · **application partnership with other third-party applications**
> 5. **Technology Maturity** — **OS, platform (server, middleware)**
> 6. **Vendor's Customer Service** — **support strength, upgrades**
> 7. **Cost of the Software** — **initial capital, AMC, scaling # users** → **TCO = Total Cost of Ownership!**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Group the criteria by what they are actually about and there are only three questions:**
>
> | Question | Criteria |
> |---|---|
> | **Does it do the job?** | **Functionality Fit · Industry Experience · Technology Maturity · Integration with 3rd party apps** |
> | **Will the vendor still be here in ten years?** | **Financial Viability · Product Strategy & Vision · Installed Base · Partnerships** |
> | **What will it really cost, and who helps us?** | **Package Cost / TCO · Customer Service & Support · Local Presence · Consulting Partnerships** |
>
> **Fit · survival · support.**
>
> **The striking thing is how few criteria are about the software itself.** Roughly half concern **the vendor as an ongoing institution**, not the product. That is not padding — it follows directly from the point the contracting slide makes: ***"ERP package procurement is not a one-time transaction with the ERP vendor. Help of the ERP vendor is needed throughout the package life cycle."***
>
> **You are not buying a product; you are entering a decade-long dependency.** Every criterion in the middle row exists because of that.

## Financial viability and installed base

> [!EXAM]
> **The question bank asks directly: "Why are financial viability and installed base important criteria for ERP package selection?"**
>
> The deck answers by listing acquisitions:
>
> $$\textbf{JD Edwards, PeopleSoft, Siebel, Retek} \;\longrightarrow\; \textbf{Oracle}$$
> $$\textbf{Manugistics} \;\longrightarrow\; \textbf{JD Edwards} \qquad\qquad \textbf{BAAN, Commerce One} \;\longrightarrow\; \textit{(gone)}$$

> [!DERIVE]
> **That list is the argument, not an illustration** — and reproducing it is how you answer the question well.
>
> **What happens to a customer when their vendor is acquired or fails:**
>
> | Outcome | Consequence for you |
> |---|---|
> | **Vendor acquired** | the product may be **merged, sunset, or put into maintenance-only** — new features stop, and eventually you are pushed onto the acquirer's platform |
> | **Vendor fails outright** | **no support, no upgrades, no patches, no compliance updates** — and you are running your entire company on it |
>
> **The switching cost is what makes this catastrophic rather than annoying.** You cannot simply change ERP the way you change a text editor: the implementation cost, the data migration, the retraining and the process redesign all have to be paid again. **Choosing the vendor is a lock-in decision, so vendor survival is a selection criterion.**
>
> **Installed base is the evidence that answers it.** A large installed base means:
> - **the vendor has revenue** — the direct financial-viability signal
> - **the product has been proven** in real companies, not just demos
> - **a market exists for skilled consultants**, so you can hire people who know it
> - **the vendor cannot abandon it** without abandoning paying customers
>
> **And it must be the *relevant* installed base**, which is why the deck qualifies it three ways — **geography/country specific, industry domain specific, MNC.** Ten thousand installations worldwide is no comfort if **none of them are in your country or your industry**: local statutory requirements will be unsupported, and no local consultant will know the product.

## Partnerships and technology maturity

> [!EXAM]
> **Partnerships — two kinds:**
> - **Implementation partnerships with consulting companies**
> - **Application partnership with other third-party applications**
>
> The question bank asks: *"What are the different types of partnerships that an ERP package vendor can have and **why is it an important criterion** for package selection?"*
>
> **Technology maturity** — *"What is meant by technology maturity of a package vendor?"* — concerns **OS, platform (server, middleware)**: which operating systems, databases and middleware the package runs on, and how proven that stack is.

> [!TRAP]
> **The two partnership types protect against two different failures, and the question bank wants both.**
>
> **Implementation partnerships** determine **whether you can get the project done at all.** A package with no consulting ecosystem leaves you dependent on the vendor's own limited services arm — expensive, capacity-constrained, and with no competitive pressure on their rates. **Many partners means you can run a competitive RFP for the implementation**, which is the entire subject of the partner-selection chapter.
>
> **Application partnerships** determine **what you can connect it to.** No ERP does everything; you will eventually bolt on a specialist CRM, an APS, a tax engine. **Pre-existing certified integrations are the difference between configuration and a custom development project.**
>
> **Both are network-effect criteria** — you are choosing an ecosystem, not a product, and the same logic explains **technology maturity**: a package that runs only on one obscure platform locks you into that platform's fate as well as the vendor's. Recall from the previous topic that **SAP "supports all leading databases"** while **JD Edwards supports DB2** — that is a maturity difference, stated as a fact about breadth.

## Cost and TCO

> [!EXAM]
> **Cost of the Software** — **initial capital · AMC · scaling # users** → **TCO = Total Cost of Ownership!**

> [!INTUITION]
> **The deck's exclamation mark on TCO is the point.** The licence price is the number on the quotation and the smallest part of what you will pay.
>
> | Cost | Nature |
> |---|---|
> | **Initial capital** — licence, hardware | **one-off**, visible on the quote |
> | **AMC** — annual maintenance contract | **recurring, forever**, typically a percentage of licence value |
> | **Scaling # users** | **grows with the company** — every new hire is a new licence |
>
> **Two of the three are recurring**, which means a package that is cheaper today can be more expensive over ten years. **AMC compounds**, and user growth compounds with the business.
>
> This is exactly Unit 1's cost analysis in miniature — where **hidden and ongoing costs** dominate the total — and it is the reason **SaaS pricing** in Unit 5 is treated as a genuinely different proposition rather than just a different payment schedule: *"the entire cost is variable and there is no fixed cost or upfront investment. **Easy to calculate ROI.**"*

## Shortlisting and final selection

> [!EXAM]
> **Initial Package Shortlisting:**
> - **Based on the above criteria, packages are given weighted scores — highest scoring shortlisted**
>
> **Final Package Selection:**
> - **Comparison chart or matrix**
> - **Functional requirements match:** **Full or direct match · Partial match · No match**
> - **Category of requirements: Vital, Essential and Desirable**
> - **Evaluation or scoring is done based on the above parameters and detailed functional requirements and the vendor's ERP offering**

> [!DERIVE]
> **The final evaluation is a two-dimensional grid, and understanding why it must be two-dimensional is the point.**
>
> $$\textbf{how well it matches} \;\times\; \textbf{how much that requirement matters}$$
>
> | | **Full match** | **Partial match** | **No match** |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **Vital** | fine | **serious problem** | **eliminate the package** |
> | **Essential** | fine | manageable | costly gap |
> | **Desirable** | bonus | fine | fine |
>
> **A one-dimensional count of matches is misleading**, and that is the whole reason for the VED categories. A package matching **900 of 1,000** requirements looks excellent — until you notice the 100 misses include something **vital**. Conversely a package missing 200 **desirable** requirements may be the right choice.
>
> **"Vital" means the business cannot operate without it, so a No-match there is disqualifying regardless of the total score.**
>
> **VED — Vital, Essential, Desirable** — is worth remembering as a named scheme; it is the requirements-prioritisation counterpart of **ABC** for inventory and of **impact categories** for risk. **The same idea in a third place: rank by consequence, then spend effort accordingly.**

## Evaluation parameters

> [!EXAM]
> **Possible parameters** — the deck's own list:
>
> **Functionality · Reliability · Flexibility · Implementation time · Total time · No process modification · No customization · Platform independence · Maintenance · Running cost · Consultancy cost · Software cost · Hardware cost · Infrastructure cost · Total cost · Training required · Availability of trained manpower · Payment terms**
>
> **And a second list of practical questions:**
>
> **Hardware support · Upgrade support for the software · Documentation · Web-enabled access · Will the software be implemented in modules? · Will the software be purchased in modules? · Year-ending accounting · Posting of transaction online · Business rules · Data entry screens · Report generation · Master data preparation · Is the customization cost included in the ERP cost?**

> [!EXAM]
> Two of the deck's evaluation slides carry **no text at all** — they are **image-only comparison matrices**, and their content is:
>
> **1. A mid-market ERP feature-comparison grid** *(Info-Tech Research Group, "Vendor Landscape: Mid-Market ERP")* scoring **Epicor, Infor, Microsoft (GP), Microsoft (NAV), NetSuite, Oracle, Sage (300), Sage (500), SAP and UNIT4** across ten features — **analytics and reporting · mobile applications · multi-site ERP functionality · integration with end-user tools · exception mgmt · industry specific modules · horizontal support · BPM support · doc. mgmt · app. dev. platform** — with each cell marked **fully present / partially present-pending / absent**.
>
> **2. A three-way capability table for SAP ERP, Oracle EBS and Microsoft Dynamics AX** across **mobile, HR, CRM, finance, IT, warehouse mgmt, eCommerce, cloud deployment, industry-specific software, supply chain, asset mgmt, procurement, processing and integration.** Its most quotable rows: **industry-specific software — SAP 25 industries, Oracle 23 industries, Microsoft Dynamics AX 4 industries**; and **eCommerce is ticked only for Dynamics AX**.

> [!TRAP]
> **The parameter lists are long on purpose, and the exam-worthy observation is what they contain.**
>
> Count them: of roughly eighteen parameters in the first list, **eight are cost or time** — *implementation time, total time, running cost, consultancy cost, software cost, hardware cost, infrastructure cost, total cost.* Only **three** are about the product's capability — *functionality, reliability, flexibility*.
>
> **Two of them are stated as negatives — "no process modification" and "no customization" — and they are scored as *good*.** That is worth pausing on: **the ideal package is the one you have to change least**, on both sides. It is the 75–80% good-fit rule appearing as a scoring line.
>
> ⚠️ **And the sharpest line in either list is the last practical question: "Is the customization cost included in the ERP cost?"** Almost always **no** — which is precisely how a package that looked cheapest at selection becomes the most expensive by go-live. **That question is the TCO warning made specific.**
