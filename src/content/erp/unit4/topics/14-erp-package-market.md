# The ERP Package Market & Its Three Tiers

## The vendor landscape

> [!EXAM]
> **ERP packages (vendors) market:**
>
> | Vendor | Products named |
> |---|---|
> | **SAP** | **All-in-One** *(for medium size companies)* · **Business One** *(for small companies)* |
> | **Oracle** | **PeopleSoft · JD Edwards · Siebel** |
> | **Others** | **Infor · IFS · Epicor · Microsoft · Ramco · other ERPs** |
>
> **For SMBs: preconfigured templates — reduces time and cost of implementation.**

> [!INTUITION]
> **The two big vendors' product lists tell two different growth stories, and noticing which is worth a mark.**
>
> **SAP's names are *tiers of its own product*** — All-in-One and Business One are SAP-built offerings aimed downward at mid-size and small companies. **SAP grew its range organically, by building smaller versions of itself.**
>
> **Oracle's names are *companies it bought*** — PeopleSoft, JD Edwards and Siebel were all independent vendors. **Oracle grew its range by acquisition.**
>
> That is exactly the list the **financial viability** criterion used as its warning:
>
> $$\textbf{JD Edwards, PeopleSoft, Siebel, Retek} \;\longrightarrow\; \textbf{Oracle}$$
>
> **Every one of Oracle's product names in this slide is a former competitor that stopped being independent.** The market slide and the viability criterion are describing the same events from two directions — one as a product catalogue, the other as a customer risk.
>
> **PeopleSoft is the sharpest example**, because the package-selection chapter opened by naming it as *"very strong in Human Resource."* **Being the best in a niche did not keep it independent.**

## The three tiers

> [!EXAM]
> **ERP market — leading players, by tier:**
>
> | Tier | Players |
> |---|---|
> | **Tier 1** | **SAP, Oracle** |
> | **Tier 2** | **Microsoft Dynamics, JD Edwards, PeopleSoft, Infor, IFS, Epicor, Lawson** *(a second slide adds **QAD**)* |
> | **Tier 3** | **Small players, local players…** *("several small ERPs")* |

> [!DERIVE]
> **The tiers are a segmentation by *customer size*, not by quality** — and treating them as a quality ranking is the standard mistake.
>
> | Tier | Typical customer | Consequence |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Tier 1** | **large enterprises, MNCs, multi-country** | broadest functionality, highest cost, longest implementations |
> | **Tier 2** | **mid-market and large single-country companies** | narrower scope, faster and cheaper to implement |
> | **Tier 3** | **small and local businesses** | often **country- or industry-specific**, minimal cost |
>
> **A Tier 1 package is frequently the *wrong* choice for a small company**, and the selection chapter already said why: **price becomes the most dominant factor for SMB**, and **TCO** — licence plus AMC plus per-user scaling — grows with the tier. Buying an ERP you cannot afford to implement is not a better outcome than buying a smaller one that works.
>
> **The reverse also holds.** A Tier 3 package will not support **multi-country statutory reporting, multi-currency consolidation, or twenty-five industry solutions** — so an MNC choosing on price alone buys a system it will outgrow before go-live.
>
> **The tier that fits is the one whose customers look like you**, which is precisely why the screening criteria demand an installed base that is **geography specific, industry domain specific, and MNC** where relevant.

> [!TRAP]
> **Note that JD Edwards and PeopleSoft appear in Tier 2 while also being listed as Oracle products.** Both statements are true and the apparent contradiction is informative: **they still exist as products and still compete in the mid-market — but the company behind them is now Oracle, a Tier 1 vendor.**
>
> This is the market consolidating: **acquisition moves a product between tiers without removing it.** For a customer, the vendor's tier changed even though the software did not — and with it the roadmap, the support model, and the upgrade path.
>
> **That is the practical content of the financial-viability criterion**: an acquisition is not usually a catastrophe, but it *is* a change of ownership of your critical system, decided without you.

## Preconfigured templates

> [!EXAM]
> **For SMBs: preconfigured templates — reduces time and cost of implementation.**

> [!INTUITION]
> **A preconfigured template is a package that arrives already configured for a typical company in a given industry** — chart of accounts, org structure, standard processes, master data structures — instead of a blank system to be configured from scratch.
>
> **Why the SMB segment specifically:**
>
> Configuration effort is **roughly fixed**, but the value it produces **scales with company size**. A six-month blueprint-and-configure exercise is proportionate for a company with twelve plants; for a company with one, **the consulting bill exceeds the licence** — and the whole project becomes uneconomic.
>
> $$\text{large firm: } \frac{\text{config cost}}{\text{benefit}} \text{ small} \qquad\qquad \text{small firm: } \frac{\text{config cost}}{\text{benefit}} \text{ large}$$
>
> **The template collapses that ratio by making the configuration a product rather than a project.**
>
> **What you give up is choice.** A template embeds *someone else's* view of how the process should run — which is fine for an SMB that has no strong opinion, and unacceptable for a company whose process is its competitive advantage. **This is the good-fit rule applied at maximum strictness: take the standard process, change nothing.**
>
> **The idea recurs twice in Unit 5.** ASAP-style **preconfigured industry solutions** are what makes **SaaS ERP** workable at all — *"success of ERP / CRM software as SaaS will depend on offering the capability of offering **industry best-practice configurations***" — and **SaaS's multi-tenant model forbids customization outright**. **The template is the SMB compromise; SaaS is that compromise made absolute.**

> [!EXAM]
> **From the image-only comparison table in the previous topic**, one row is worth carrying here as evidence for the tier structure:
>
> | | **SAP ERP** | **Oracle EBS** | **Microsoft Dynamics AX** |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **Industry-specific software** | **25 industries** | **23 industries** | **4 industries** |
>
> **Two Tier 1 vendors with 25 and 23 industry solutions; a Tier 2 vendor with 4.** That single row is the tier distinction measured — **breadth of industry coverage is what Tier 1 is actually selling**, and it is why *"industry specific functionality"* can override the entire selection process for companies in the industries only Tier 1 covers.
