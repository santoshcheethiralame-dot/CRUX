# Extended ERP — SPACES

## The six applications

> [!EXAM]
> The **extended state of the ERP system to Enterprise Application Systems**:
>
> | Abbrev. | Full name |
> |---|---|
> | **SCM** | **Supply Chain Management** |
> | **CRM** | **Customer Relationship Management** |
> | **PLM** | **Product Lifecycle Management** |
> | **APS** | **Advanced Planning and Scheduling** |
> | **SRM** | **Supplier Relationship Management** |
> | **EAM** | **Enterprise Asset Management** |
>
> The deck elsewhere adds **BPM — Business Process Management** to the extended list.

> [!INTUITION]
> **Mnemonic — the six spell SPACES:**
>
> | Letter | Application | It manages… |
> |---|---|---|
> | **S** | **SCM** | the flow of **goods** |
> | **P** | **PLM** | the life of a **product** |
> | **A** | **APS** | **planning and scheduling** |
> | **C** | **CRM** | **customers** |
> | **E** | **EAM** | physical **equipment / assets** |
> | **S** | **SRM** | **suppliers** |
>
> Fitting, because these are exactly the **spaces around ERP** — ERP sits in the middle and these six extend it outward.
>
> Better still, they pair off naturally: **CRM and SRM** are the two ends of the chain (customers and suppliers), **SCM and APS** are both about flow and timing, and **PLM and EAM** are both lifecycle managers — one for the product you sell, one for the equipment you own.

## SCM — Supply Chain Management

> [!EXAM]
> **SCM is the oversight of materials, information, and finances as they move in a process from supplier → manufacturer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer.**
>
> It involves **coordinating and integrating these flows both within and among companies/users.**
>
> **Goal:** to **reduce cost of inventory** and enable **efficient movement of materials (supply)**.
>
> **Example:** Amazon uses SCM to ensure timely product delivery and efficient inventory management.

> [!TRAP]
> Note that **three things flow**, not one: **materials, information and finances.** Students reliably remember materials and forget the other two.
>
> The information flow is arguably the important one — knowing *where* the goods are is what allows the materials flow to be optimised at all. And the five-link chain **supplier → manufacturer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer** is worth memorising in order, since questions ask you to place SCM's scope.

## CRM — Customer Relationship Management

> [!EXAM]
> **CRM is a strategy for managing a company's interactions with its customers and sales prospects.**
>
> - Often CRM is equated with **automating business processes related to sales, marketing and customer service — but CRM is not just about technology.**
> - It involves **strategy and redesign of business processes**, with the overall goal to **find, attract and win new customers, retain those customers, and finally get feedback from them** to better design the company's product and customer-facing processes.

> [!INTUITION]
> The CRM goal is a **four-stage lifecycle** and it is easy to hold as a sentence:
>
> $$\textbf{Find} \rightarrow \textbf{Attract} \rightarrow \textbf{Win} \rightarrow \textbf{Retain} \rightarrow \textbf{Feedback}$$
>
> The **feedback** step is the one worth calling out: it loops back into product design, which makes CRM a source of requirements and not merely a sales tool.
>
> **"CRM is not just about technology"** is the same argument the course makes about ERP itself — *a business strategy*, not a software purchase. Expect it as a short-answer question.

### CRM application areas

> [!EXAM]
> The nine areas on the deck's CRM diagram:
>
> **Sales Force Automation · Marketing · Revenue and Pricing · Product Configuration · Order Management · CRM Analytics · Field Service · e-Commerce · Call Centre · Partner Relationship Management**

> [!INTUITION]
> Group them by **where the customer is** and the list becomes recallable:
>
> - **Before the sale** — Marketing, Sales Force Automation, Product Configuration, Revenue & Pricing
> - **At the sale** — Order Management, e-Commerce
> - **After the sale** — Field Service, Call Centre
> - **Around it all** — CRM Analytics, Partner Relationship Management
>
> Note **Order Management appears in CRM as well as in ERP's O2C process.** That overlap is real and is why CRM is listed as an ERP module in the O2C table — the boundary between core and extended ERP is genuinely blurred.

## PLM — Product Lifecycle Management

> [!EXAM]
> **PLM is a new set of enterprise applications that manages all data and information about a product from its initial conception to retirement.**
>
> The product lifecycle includes **conceptualization of the product, design, manufacturing, ensuring quality throughout its life cycle, ensuring product safety during manufacturing, distribution and usage, and finally service — from concept to development till the product retires from service.**
>
> **Products may have a short (market) life cycle or a prolonged one:** *a winter fashion **or** a ship engine.*

> [!EXAM]
> **The four-stage Product Life Cycle curve** on the deck's diagram, plotting **Revenue and Profit against Time**:
>
> | Stage | Revenue | Profit |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Introduction** | low, starting to rise | **negative or near zero** |
> | **Growth** | rising steeply | rising |
> | **Maturity** | **peaks and flattens** | **peaks, then begins to fall** |
> | **Decline** | falling | falling toward zero |
>
> **Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline.**

> [!DERIVE]
> **Read the two curves against each other — that is where the exam value is.**
>
> **Profit lags revenue and peaks earlier.** In *Introduction*, revenue exists but development and launch costs exceed it, so profit is negative. In *Maturity*, revenue is still at its highest but profit has already turned down — competition has forced prices and margins lower.
>
> **The implication for PLM:** the moment to start developing the *next* product is during **Maturity**, while the current one is still earning. Wait until Decline and there is no revenue left to fund the replacement.
>
> This is also why **Time to Market (T2M)** is a named process and PLM is its ERP module — **shortening T2M shifts the whole curve left and buys more profitable years.**

> [!INTUITION]
> The **"winter fashion or a ship engine"** contrast is the point of the slide. A fashion line's entire curve runs for one season; a ship engine's runs for **decades**, with service and spare parts long after manufacture stops.
>
> **PLM must handle both**, which is why it covers *service* and *retirement* and not just design — for the ship engine, most of the lifecycle happens after the sale.

## APS, SRM and EAM

> [!EXAM]
> | | Definition | Example |
> |---|---|---|
> | **APS** — Advanced Planning and Scheduling | Optimizes **production planning, resource allocation and scheduling** in manufacturing. Helps **forecast demand, balance production capacity and improve delivery performance** | A manufacturer schedules machine operations and workforce efficiently to meet customer orders on time |
> | **SRM** — Supplier Relationship Management | Manages relationships with suppliers: **supplier selection, performance evaluation, contract management, procurement and collaboration**, ensuring timely delivery of quality materials at competitive prices | A retail company evaluates supplier performance and manages purchase agreements |
> | **EAM** — Enterprise Asset Management | Manages **physical assets throughout their lifecycle**: monitor asset performance, schedule **preventive maintenance**, reduce equipment downtime, extend asset life | A power plant manages turbines, generators and maintenance schedules for uninterrupted operations |

> [!TRAP]
> **APS is the descendant of closed-loop MRP** — both are about checking a plan against real capacity. If a question asks what APS adds over basic ERP planning, the answer is **optimisation under finite capacity constraints**, which is exactly the gap plain MRP left open.
>
> Do not confuse **SRM with SCM**. **SRM manages the *relationship* with suppliers** (selection, contracts, performance); **SCM manages the *flow* of goods** through the whole chain. One is commercial, the other logistical.

## ERP and Enterprise Application Systems

> [!EXAM]
> **ERP is a subset — a large one!** It **covers all the basic transactions inside the organization and dictates the overall business framework.**
>
> The extended state adds **CRM, SCM, PLM, BPM and SRM** around it.

> [!INTUITION]
> Hold the architecture as three rings:
>
> 1. **Core ERP** — HCM, Finance, Procurement, Inventory, Production Planning. *Inside the company, transactional.*
> 2. **Extended ERP (SPACES)** — SCM, PLM, APS, CRM, EAM, SRM. *Reaching outward and forward in time.*
> 3. **EBS** — the whole family, including Business Intelligence.
>
> Every exam question about "where does X fit" is answered by naming the ring.
