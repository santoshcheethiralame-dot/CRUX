# Challenges & Risks of ERP Implementation

## The three categories

> [!EXAM]
> **Three categories of challenges:**
>
> 1. **Implementation Challenges**
> 2. **Support / Maintenance Challenges**
> 3. **Managing People Challenges**
>
> This is a **stated question-bank item** — *"What are the three major categories of ERP challenges?"*

> [!INTUITION]
> **Mnemonic — "I See People" (I-S-P):**
>
> - **I** — **Implementation** *(building it)*
> - **S** — **Support / maintenance** *(running it)*
> - **P** — **People** *(everyone affected by it)*
>
> The three map neatly onto the life cycle: **I** covers phases 1–5, **S** covers phase 6, and **P** runs across all of them. And the ordering carries the lesson — **people come last in the list and first in importance**, which is the *socio-technical system* point.

## A) Implementation challenges

> [!EXAM]
> **Six items:**
> 1. **Scope changes**
> 2. **Huge budget**
> 3. **Long timeline**
> 4. **Process redesign**
> 5. **Unrealistic expectation**
> 6. **Complex interfaces**

> [!EXAM]
> The deck's elaboration on each:
>
> | Challenge | Detail |
> |---|---|
> | **Scope** | **Never gets finalized · analysis paralysis · conflicting needs & priorities** |
> | **Long timeline** | **Difficult to sustain momentum · benefits/results cannot be delayed beyond a point** |
> | **Business process redesign** | **Many implementations driven purely by BPR have failed** |
> | **Expectation management & infeasible deadline** | **ERP cannot solve all the problems of the company** · dependencies are high and the project team needs a lot of time · **matching the benefits delivered with the initial commitments** |
> | **Complex interfaces** | ERP interfaces to several other systems; **depending on the organization's IT landscape, complexity of integration can be high** |

> [!TRAP]
> **"Many implementations driven purely by BPR have failed"** is a surprising line, given that the course sells BPR as a benefit. The operative word is **purely**.
>
> An implementation that treats ERP as an excuse to redesign every process at once takes on **two enormous changes simultaneously** — new software *and* new ways of working — and the combined change exceeds what the organisation can absorb. **The failure is one of dosage, not of direction.**
>
> Note the connection: the deployment strategies with **High change management** load are exactly the **High risk** ones.

> [!DERIVE]
> **Scope is listed first and reappears everywhere**, and it is worth tracing why:
>
> - It is **implementation challenge #1** (*"never gets finalized"*)
> - **Scope freezing** is an activity in **Pre-Implementation**
> - **"Scope creeps"** is a named example under **risk management**
> - Scope drives **budget** and **timeline**, which are challenges #2 and #3
>
> **Uncontrolled scope is the root cause of the two challenges that follow it.** If an exam asks for the single biggest implementation challenge with justification, scope is the defensible answer.

## B) Support / maintenance challenges

> [!EXAM]
> **Six items:**
> 1. **Getting the right people**
> 2. **Managing multiple vendors**
> 3. **Managing regular updates**
> 4. **Technology obsolescence**
> 5. **Large application portfolio**
> 6. **Realizing benefits**

> [!EXAM]
> The deck's elaboration:
> - **Right people for the support job**
> - **Vendor management for SLA**
> - **Upgrades — of the ERP, and of the technology**
> - **Maintaining synchronization with other enterprise app life cycles**

> [!INTUITION]
> **"Realizing benefits" sitting in the *support* list is the entry worth noticing.**
>
> The benefits are not delivered at go-live — they accrue **during operation**, and only if the system is actually used well. Which is why it is a *challenge*: the project team has disbanded, the excitement is over, and someone must still ensure the promised inventory reduction actually happens.
>
> This links straight to Chapter 3's **business case tracking**: *"though cost benefit analysis is done in the beginning, actuals might differ — need to track it at the right milestones."*

> [!TRAP]
> **"Maintaining synchronization with other enterprise app life cycles"** is the subtle one. Your ERP, your CRM and your SCM are all separately upgraded by separate vendors on separate schedules.
>
> **Upgrading one can break its interface to another** — which is *"complex interfaces"* from the implementation list, now recurring forever. A one-off integration problem becomes a permanent coordination problem.

## C) Managing people challenges

> [!EXAM]
> **Four items:**
> 1. **Managing change**
> 2. **Managing large project team**
> 3. **Managing employee retention and relocation**
> 4. **Top management support**

> [!EXAM]
> The elaboration:
> - **Managing change** — *a later topic in the next unit*
> - **Large team management** — **multi-location / countries**, **creation, disbanding, relocating**, **extending core team for support**
> - **Top management support** — **to ensure right priority is given**, and to **resolve issues of co-operation amongst seniors**

> [!INTUITION]
> **"Resolve issues of co-operation amongst seniors"** is the most honest line in the chapter.
>
> An ERP implementation forces departments to give up their own systems and adopt shared processes — and that means **department heads lose autonomy**. No project manager can compel a peer to cooperate; only **top management** can. This is why top-management support appears in the very first slide of the life cycle chapter *and* here.
>
> It is also the mechanism behind Nestlé's rebellion: *"the new business process confused most of employees, then resistance grew into rebellion in 2000."*

## The risk statistics

> [!EXAM]
> **ERP implementation is full of risks.** The **Standish Report (around 1998)** snapshot on success/failure:
>
> - **10% of ERP implementations succeed**
> - **Cost overruns average 178%**
> - **Schedule overruns average 230%**
> - **Implemented functionality averages 41% of what is desired**
>
> **Another survey — 51% of implementers felt unsuccessful.**

> [!DERIVE]
> **Read the four numbers as one sentence:** the typical ERP project costs **nearly three times** its budget, takes **over three times** as long, and delivers **less than half** of what was asked for — and only **one in ten** succeeds outright.
>
> The two overrun figures are quotable as multipliers: *178% overrun* means final cost ≈ **2.78×** budget; *230% overrun* means **3.3×** the schedule.
>
> These figures justify everything else in the chapter — the insistence on methodology, on risk management, on top-management support, and on Nestlé's *"don't start a project with a deadline in mind."*

## Further risks

> [!EXAM]
> - **Customization challenges**
> - **Delayed payback period** — **average ROI in the first year could be as little as 5%; ROI takes 3 years for most (Meta Group)**
> - **Data quality loss** — **only 1 out of 3 companies are sure of their data quality. Garbage In — Garbage Out!**
> - **Implementation complexity**

> [!TRAP]
> **The delayed payback figure is the one that kills business cases.** *5% ROI in year one, full ROI at three years* — and it explains why Chapter 3 computes costs over a **3–5 year horizon** rather than annually.
>
> It also explains the *"long timeline"* challenge from the other end: the organisation must fund a large negative cash flow for years before the return appears. A CFO looking only at year one will always conclude ERP is a bad investment.

> [!INTUITION]
> **"Garbage In — Garbage Out"** deserves its own thought. ERP integration means every module reads the **same** data — so **one bad master record is now wrong everywhere at once**.
>
> Before integration, a bad vendor address damaged one department's file. After integration, it damages procurement, receiving, invoicing and payment simultaneously. **Integration amplifies data quality problems as much as it amplifies data quality benefits** — which is why data migration and cleansing is a named Go Live activity rather than a technicality.

## Hidden costs

> [!EXAM]
> **Hidden costs / issues (come as a surprise):**
> - **Training**
> - **Integration testing**
> - **Data analysis and conversion**
> - **Consultants ad infinitum**
> - **Typically pulls out "good" resources from other projects/functions** — and **team pull-back takes a long time**

> [!INTUITION]
> The last item is the **indirect cost** of Chapter 3 appearing here as a risk: *"internal employee cost"* and *"operational disruption cost"*.
>
> The mechanism is worth stating: an ERP project needs the people who **understand the business best** — and those are exactly the people whose day jobs suffer most in their absence. **You cannot staff an ERP project with people you can spare.** And *"team pull-back takes a long time"*: having spent two years on ERP, they are no longer current in their old roles.
>
> **"Consultants ad infinitum"** is the cost-side version: consultants are engaged for the implementation, then retained for support, then for the upgrade — a temporary expense that becomes permanent.

## Risk management

> [!EXAM]
> **The six-step risk management process:**
>
> 1. **Identify risks**
> 2. **Analyze impact of risks**
> 3. **Assess probability of risk occurring**
> 4. **Prioritize risks**
> 5. **Mitigation plan creation**
> 6. **Contingency plan creation**
>
> **Examples of risks:** **scope creeps · technology not stable / available at all locations · employee comfort on technology/use.**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Steps 2 and 3 are the two axes of every risk matrix** — **impact** and **probability** — and step 4 ranks by their combination. That is the same structure as a security risk assessment: *likelihood × impact → rating.*
>
> **Steps 5 and 6 are a pair and are not the same thing:**
>
> | | Purpose | Acts |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Mitigation** | **Reduce** the probability or impact | **before** the risk occurs |
> | **Contingency** | **Cope** if it happens anyway | **after** it occurs |
>
> You need both, because mitigation never reduces probability to zero. The Go Live checklist shows the pair in practice: **parallel runs** are mitigation; **"contingency plans for the cut-over"** are contingency.
>
> **Mnemonic: "I Analyse, Assess, Prioritise, Mitigate, Contingency" — I-A-A-P-M-C.**
