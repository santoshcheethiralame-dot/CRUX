# Benefits & ROI of ERP

## The business case

> [!EXAM]
> - **A business case compares an initiative based on investments and returns on it — ROI**
> - **Establishing an ROI for ERP investment is important before starting the project, as ERP is a huge investment**
> - **It is easier to calculate cost for ERP**, as the same can be estimated quickly with estimates from different vendors
> - **Benefit calculation is always a challenge**, as this is generally **based on lots of assumptions** and benefits could be **intangible and long term**

> [!INTUITION]
> **The asymmetry is the whole difficulty:** costs are **quotable** — you ask three vendors and get three numbers. Benefits are **predicted** — you assume an inventory reduction that has not happened yet.
>
> So an ERP business case is **a precise number on one side and an estimate on the other**, and the estimate is the side that justifies the project. This is also the *co-benefits* problem from Chapter 1: even after the fact, you cannot cleanly attribute the gain to ERP alone.

## The four tangible (direct) benefits

> [!EXAM]
> **Direct benefits:**
> 1. **Inventory Reduction**
> 2. **Better Customer Service and Reduced Lost Sales**
> 3. **Reducing Number of Days of Outstanding**
> 4. **Material and Labor Cost Reductions**
>
> **Types of benefits:** **Tangibles and Intangibles** · **Quantitative and Qualitative**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Mnemonic — the four tangible benefits spell I-C-O-M ("I COMe out ahead"):**
>
> | Letter | Benefit | Where the money comes from |
> |---|---|---|
> | **I** | **Inventory** reduction | less capital tied up in stock |
> | **C** | **Customer** service / reduced lost sales | more revenue captured |
> | **O** | **Outstanding** days reduced | cash arrives sooner |
> | **M** | **Material and labour** cost reduction | lower input costs |
>
> Notice that **three of the four are about cash timing, not profit**: inventory, days outstanding and material costs all release **working capital**. Only "reduced lost sales" adds revenue. **ERP's financial case is largely a working-capital case** — which is exactly what the worked example below shows.

### 1 — Inventory reduction

> [!EXAM]
> - **Better management of stock at different locations and quantities**
> - **Handle expiry dated / perishable items better**
> - **Manage slow moving items / dead stock**
> - **Manage item availability based on BOM**
> - **Control stock levels based on orders / forecasting**

### 2 — Better customer service and reduced lost sales

> [!EXAM]
> - **Minimize lost orders**
> - **Availability in full/part check — no wrong promise**
> - **Maintain min stock levels — avoiding stock-outs**
> - **Quicker planning ability for short demand surges**

> [!TRAP]
> **"No wrong promise"** is the mechanism worth understanding. Without integration, Sales promises a delivery date **without knowing** whether stock exists — the Dell failure from Chapter 1. With ERP, the availability check happens **at the moment of the promise**.
>
> The benefit is therefore **not just more sales — it is fewer broken commitments**, which is why it is phrased as *customer service* and not simply *revenue*.

### 3 — Reducing number of days outstanding

> [!EXAM]
> - **Accurate and timely invoicing — less disputes**
> - **Tracking of due, overdue invoices and actions thereof — reminders, "dunning"**
> - **Link up with order entry to manage defaulting / delayed customers**
> - **Reduces risks of currency fluctuation impacts in cross-border transactions**
>
> **Days outstanding is one of the measures of health of a business — statutory reports show them.**

> [!INTUITION]
> **"Dunning"** is the technical term for systematically chasing overdue payments, and it is worth knowing by name — it appears again in the worked example.
>
> The clever item is the third: **link up with order entry**. The system can **refuse or flag a new order from a customer who has not paid for the last one** — impossible without integration, because Sales and Accounts Receivable would be looking at different systems. **This is the O2C cycle closing the loop on itself.**

### 4 — Material and labour cost reduction

> [!EXAM]
> - **Consolidated requirements to help volume transactions**
> - **Better vendor supply schedule and no emergency costs**
> - **Forecasts support buying at best of times**
> - **Less overtime, less idle time**
> - **Adjust capacity & schedules**

> [!INTUITION]
> **"Consolidated requirements"** is the Nestlé lesson in general form. If four divisions each buy vanilla separately, each pays a small-order price — **29 different prices to the same vendor.** Consolidate the requirement and you negotiate once, at volume.
>
> And **"less overtime, less idle time"** are the *same* problem in two directions: both are symptoms of capacity not matching demand, which is what planning fixes.

## Intangible benefits

> [!EXAM]
> **Intangible benefits:**
> - **Easy data entry**
> - **Information readily available — wherever, whenever, to whoever**
> - **Best practices** implementation, as followed by leading companies, coming as part of the ERP package
> - **Quicker operations & transactions — quote to cash cycle**
> - **Staff morale**
> - **Inter-org collaboration**
> - **Intra-org communication**
> - **Better data management**
> - **Better / improved customer service**
>
> The deck's diagram adds: **Online real-time information · Facilitates BPR · Integration · Reduce quote-to-cash cycle time · Automate transactions.**

> [!EXAM]
> **Intangible / qualitative benefits** from the business-case slide:
> - **Increased turnover**
> - **Better corporate image**
> - **Lower risk on money matters**
> - **Better employee morale**
>
> **How to value these? Subjective.**
>
> **When ERP is mandated by a customer, it becomes quite tangible.**

> [!TRAP]
> That last line is the exam-worthy twist. **Some business customers mandate use of ERP** to facilitate business ease — for **demand collaboration, electronic payment and standard documentation.**
>
> When that happens, the benefit stops being a soft "better collaboration" and becomes **binary and entirely tangible: without ERP you lose the customer.** The value of the benefit equals the value of the account.
>
> **An intangible benefit can become tangible when it becomes a precondition for doing business.**

## The worked business case

> [!EXAM]
> **Company ABC** — **Rs. 2,000 Cr turnover** (just-concluded financial year), projected ERP cost **Rs. 19.14 Cr** over **1 + 5 years**.
>
> **Projected benefit parameters and rationale:**
>
> | Parameter | Rationale | Extent of benefit |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Labor cost saving** | Remove / re-organize activities; same person can do more | **2%** |
> | **Material cost saving** | Better negotiated purchase; focus on high-cost material; economies of scale; better forecasting — JIT | **1%** |
> | **Finance cost** | Reduced inventory cost; synchronization across stages in spending | **interest cost reduction @ 12%** |
> | **Debtors** | Track regularly — **dunning** | **outstanding 50 days → 45 days** |

> [!DERIVE]
> **The benefits computation, line by line:**
>
> **1 — Labour:** 2% of 200 Cr → **4 Cr**
>
> **2 — Material:** 1% of 1000 Cr → **1 Cr**
>
> **3 — Finance cost savings from inventory:**
> Inventory turnover improves from **5 → 6**.
> - Value at 5 turns = **400 Cr**
> - Value at 6 turns = **333 Cr**
> - Reduction = **67 Cr**
> - Finance saving @ 12% → **8 Cr**
>
> **4 — Better debt management:**
> Working capital reduced from **68.4 → 54.79**; interest on the saved **13.6 @ 12%** → **1.62 Cr**
>
> $$\textbf{Total} = 4 + 1 + 8 + 1.62 = \mathbf{23.62\ Crore}$$

> [!INTUITION]
> **Compare the two numbers and the case makes itself:**
>
> $$\textbf{Cost} = 19.14\ \text{Cr over 6 years} \qquad \textbf{Benefit} = 23.62\ \text{Cr per year}$$
>
> The annual benefit **exceeds the entire six-year cost**. On these assumptions the project repays itself in **under a year of steady-state operation** — though recall the Meta Group caution that **year-one ROI may be as little as 5%**, because the benefits ramp up while the costs are front-loaded.
>
> **The largest single benefit is item 3 — 8 Cr from inventory turnover**, and note it is *not* a saving on the inventory itself. It is the **interest no longer paid on the capital that was tied up in it.** Turnover rising from 5 to 6 releases 67 Cr of cash, and 12% of 67 is 8.
>
> **This is the working-capital point from I-C-O-M, in numbers.**

> [!TRAP]
> **Inventory turnover arithmetic is worth being able to redo.** Turnover = cost of goods sold ÷ average inventory, so **higher turnover means less stock held for the same sales**:
>
> $$\text{Inventory} = \frac{2000}{5} = 400 \qquad \rightarrow \qquad \frac{2000}{6} = 333$$
>
> **The same business, the same sales, 67 Cr less cash sitting on shelves.** If an exam gives you a turnover improvement, this is the computation it wants.

> [!EXAM]
> **The exercise the deck sets** — worth attempting:
>
> > Compare the investment and benefits for a **services company**: ERP cost **4 crore** · **120,000 transactions per year** · billing **Rs. 400** per transaction · profit **Rs. 80** per transaction · transactions **increase 40%** · transaction cost **reduced by 20%**. **What could be the intangibles?**

## Tracking the business case

> [!EXAM]
> - **Though cost-benefit analysis is done in the beginning, actuals might differ**
> - **Need to track it at the right milestones**
> - **Deviations can be analyzed and learning can be ploughed back into activities during the support phase**

> [!INTUITION]
> This closes the loop with the support challenge **"realizing benefits."** A business case written once and never revisited is a **sales document**, not a management tool.
>
> Tracking at milestones is what turns it into one — and it is the same discipline as Nestlé's *"update your budget projection at regular intervals."*
