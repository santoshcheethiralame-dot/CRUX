# Why Organizations Need ERP

## Organizations grow into departments

> [!NOTE]
> **Organizations start small and evolve as a network of comprehensive functional units or departments.** The deck's list:
>
> **Manufacturing · Sales & Distribution · Finance and Accounting · Quality Management · Logistics Management · Service Management · HR · Strategic Planning & Management · R&D · Marketing & Public Relations · Information Technology (IT) · Facilities and Administration**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Departments are not a mistake — they are a necessary response to growth.** One person can run a shop; ten thousand people cannot all do everything, so the organisation specialises.
>
> The trouble is that specialisation was allowed to extend to the **systems and the targets** as well as the work. Each department bought its own software and was measured on its own numbers, and *that* is what ERP undoes — **not the departments themselves.**

## The Dell thought experiment

> [!EXAM]
> Consider a company like **Dell Technologies** — thousands of employees, millions of customers. Every day it manages:
>
> **Receiving customer orders · Procuring raw materials · Manufacturing laptops and servers · Managing inventory · Paying suppliers · Processing employee salaries · Tracking shipments · Preparing financial reports · Handling customer support**
>
> **Now imagine managing all these activities using separate software applications. Would it work efficiently?**

The deck's own answer, worked through one order for 1,000 laptops:

> [!DERIVE]
> **Without an integrated system:**
> - Sales confirms the order — **Manufacturing is unaware**, because Sales uses a different system.
> - **Procurement doesn't know** additional processors need to be purchased.
> - **Finance doesn't know** whether the customer is credit-approved.
> - **Warehouse doesn't know** inventory requirements.
>
> **Result: delayed delivery · duplicate data entry · communication gaps · poor customer satisfaction.**
>
> **With ERP:** *"the order is entered **once**, and every department automatically receives the information relevant to its role."* Inventory, production schedules, financial records, shipping status and customer information update **in real time**.

## The classic conflict: Production vs Sales

This is the single most examinable example in Chapter 1, and it is worth being able to reproduce end to end.

> [!EXAM]
> **Till around the 1990s** the focus was to **compartmentalize and specialize departments**, and to **set individual targets for higher productivity and efficiency**. But **targets across departments were often not in sync**:
>
> $$\textbf{Production} \rightarrow \textbf{Quantity} \qquad\qquad \textbf{Sales} \rightarrow \textbf{Variety}$$
>
> **Capacity planning was not done**, so there was a **need for consolidated planning at enterprise level**.

> [!DERIVE]
> **Why each department wants what it wants.**
>
> **Production is measured on cost per unit.** To lower it, it prefers **large batches, standardized products, fewer variations, continuous runs** — because a single product run repeatedly **reduces machine setup time, reduces labour effort, improves efficiency and lowers production cost.**
>
> > Water bottles: Production wants **10,000 blue bottles**, not 2,000 each of blue, red, green, black and white.
>
> **Sales is measured on revenue.** Customers want **different colours, sizes, models and customizations**, so more options make it easier to attract different customers.
>
> > Mobile phones: customers want black / blue / white / green **and** 64 GB / 128 GB / 256 GB.
>
> **The conflict, stated plainly:**
>
> | | Production goal | Sales goal |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Quantity** | High | Low |
> | **Variety** | Low | High |
>
> **Both departments are doing their jobs correctly. Their objectives are simply opposite.**

> [!TRAP]
> The examinable point is **not** that one department is wrong. The deck is explicit: *"Both departments are trying to do their jobs effectively, but their objectives are opposite."*
>
> This is **local optimization defeating global optimization** — each department hits its target while the *enterprise* loses. The deck's other illustration of the same thing:
>
> > Production produces **50,000 units** — target achieved. Sales can sell only **30,000**. Result: **20,000 units unsold.** Production met its target and the business made a loss.

> [!NOTE]
> **Without ERP**, Sales accepts orders for many variants but Production may not have the capacity or materials. Result: **delivery delays, excess inventory, customer dissatisfaction, increased costs, poor coordination.**
>
> **With ERP**, the sales forecast flows automatically to Production Planning, Inventory Management and Procurement:
>
> **Customer demand → Sales forecast → ERP → Production planning → Material planning → Manufacturing**

> [!INTUITION]
> **The pizza-restaurant version**, which the deck offers for the viva: the kitchen wants to make **one** type of pizza because it is faster; customers want Margherita, Veggie, Paneer and Pepperoni. **The kitchen prefers quantity, the customer prefers variety.**
>
> ERP does not *abolish* the conflict — nothing can, because it is a real trade-off. What it does is **let both sides plan against the same numbers**, so the compromise is made deliberately at enterprise level instead of discovered too late.

## The enterprise scenario

> [!EXAM]
> The problems the deck lists when departments run their own systems:
>
> - **Purchase department information not available / compatible** with the needs of Production
> - **Neither does it link up with Finance or HR**
> - **A variety of systems creates IT maintenance nightmares**
> - **Duplication of data and processing infrastructure** — *example: vendor details stored in multiple systems*
>
> **What is the problem?**
> - **Optimal planning is difficult without integration**
> - **Hard to change — compatibility issues**

> [!EXAM]
> A **delay in one function has a cascading effect**: *a design delay in R&D hits product shipment in Logistics.* This demands **much higher flexibility across functions to support each other**, because **process efficiency is more important than functional KPIs**.

## The ten problems of disintegrated data

> [!EXAM]
> **Major challenges — problems with disintegrated data:**
>
> 1. **Duplicate data entry**
> 2. **Data inconsistency**
> 3. **Poor communication**
> 4. **Delayed information sharing**
> 5. **Reduced productivity**
> 6. **Increased operational costs**
> 7. **Manual reconciliation**
> 8. **Difficulty in decision-making**
> 9. **Difficulty in compliance and auditing**
> 10. **Reduced customer satisfaction**

> [!INTUITION]
> Ten items is a lot to recall cold. They are really **one root cause and its consequences**, so remember the chain rather than the list:
>
> $$\textbf{Duplicate entry} \rightarrow \textbf{inconsistency} \rightarrow \textbf{manual reconciliation} \rightarrow \textbf{delay} \rightarrow \textbf{bad decisions} \rightarrow \textbf{unhappy customers}$$
>
> Everything else — poor communication, lost productivity, higher costs, audit difficulty — hangs off that spine. If you can tell the story, you can regenerate the list.

## The "Multi" problems of modern enterprises

> [!EXAM]
> **Organization evolution creates the "Multi" problems:**
>
> - **Multi-Division / Product**
> - **Multi-Location / Facilities**
> - **Multi-Country**
> - **Multi-Currency**
> - **Multi-Lingual**
> - **Multi-Cultural**
> - **Multi (mixed) mode manufacturing**
> - **24 × 7 × 365 operations**

> [!INTUITION]
> Seven "Multi"s plus a clock. The trick is that they come in an **expanding sequence** — each one is a consequence of the previous:
>
> **Grow your products → you need multiple divisions. Grow beyond one city → multiple locations. Cross a border → country, then currency, then language, then culture, all at once.** And once you span time zones, somebody is always working, so the system can never be taken offline — **24 × 7 × 365**.
>
> **Say "Division, Location, Country — then Currency, Language, Culture come free with the border."** That plus mixed-mode manufacturing and the always-on clock gives you all eight.

> [!TRAP]
> The last item is easy to skip and is genuinely different in kind. **24 × 7 × 365 is not a "multi" at all — it is an availability requirement**, and it is what rules out systems that need a nightly batch window. It is also why ERP upgrades are so painful: there is no convenient time to stop.
