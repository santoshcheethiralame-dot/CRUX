# Implementation Methodologies — ASAP & Ascendant

## Why methodology decides the outcome

> [!EXAM]
> - **ERP adoption has equal emphasis on product and the implementation**
> - **Since the product is stable and "available", success solely depends on good implementation**
> - **Implementation expert companies command a premium, hence!**
>
> **Methodology = a set of rules, methods, tools to achieve a specific goal in a specific way.**

> [!EXAM]
> The question bank's fuller definition:
>
> **A methodology is a process followed for an implementation. It is a set of steps to be carried out in a particular sequence which has a scientific approach to make the implementation successful when followed judiciously. It generally has evolved over time with a list of proven activities, deliverables, templates and accelerators to facilitate any ERP implementation.** When designed and applied for a specific client environment, **the methodology provides the guidance, a framework and process check points that drive the project team to succeed.**

> [!INTUITION]
> The argument in the first three bullets is worth spelling out because it explains the entire consulting industry:
>
> **Everyone can buy the same SAP.** The software is a commodity — stable, available, identical for every customer. **Therefore the software cannot be the differentiator.** What differs between a successful and a failed implementation is **how it was implemented** — and that is why implementation firms *"command a premium."*
>
> The same logic explains why **methodologies have brand names**. If implementation is the thing being sold, the method *is* the product.

> [!EXAM]
> **Implementation methodologies got developed through 100s of implementation best practices.** Note the direction: methods are **distilled from experience**, not designed in advance — which is exactly what the *"harvest knowledge"* step of implementation closure feeds.

> [!EXAM]
> | Owner | Methodology |
> |---|---|
> | **SAP** | **"ASAP"** |
> | **PwC / IBM** | **"Ascendant"** |
> | **Oracle** | **"AIM" / "Fast Forward"** |
> | **Scala / Epicor** | **"Signature"** |
>
> **Has a brand value!**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Mnemonic — pair each methodology with its vendor by sound:**
>
> - **A**SAP → **S**AP *(the name contains it)*
> - **A**scendant → **A**ccounting firm *(PwC)*, with IBM
> - **AIM** → **Oracle** *(Application Implementation Method)*
> - **Signature** → **Scala/Epicor** *(S and S)*
>
> The one you must not miss is **ASAP = SAP**, because the MCQ bank tests it directly and offers Oracle, PeopleSoft and Baan as distractors.

## ASAP — Accelerated SAP

> [!EXAM]
> **ASAP is the Accelerated SAP methodology Roadmap.**
>
> **Objectives:**
> 1. **Quick and cost effective implementation**
> 2. **Maximize resource utilization**
> 3. **Involve users community**
> 4. **Process oriented**
> 5. **Re-usability**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Mnemonic for the five ASAP objectives — "Quick Managers Involve Process Reuse":**
>
> | Word | Objective |
> |---|---|
> | **Q**uick | Quick and cost effective |
> | **M**anagers | Maximize resource utilization |
> | **I**nvolve | Involve users community |
> | **P**rocess | Process oriented |
> | **R**euse | Re-usability |
>
> Note the name itself carries objective 1: **"Accelerated"** SAP. If you remember nothing else, **ASAP's headline promise is speed** — which is also its stated risk, since a fast implementation is one that has less time to manage change.

### ASAP tools

> [!EXAM]
> **The five ASAP tools:**
>
> | Tool | What it does |
> |---|---|
> | **Roadmap composer** | Assembles the phase-by-phase roadmap |
> | **Q&A database** | Structured questions used to capture requirements |
> | **Project IMG** | **Implementation Guide** |
> | **Transports** | **Configuration move from dev. to deployment** |
> | **BC Sets** | **Configuration recorder** |

> [!DERIVE]
> **The tools map onto the phases**, which is the neatest way to remember them:
>
> - **Roadmap composer** → planning *(Project Preparation)*
> - **Q&A database** → gathering requirements *(Blueprinting)*
> - **Project IMG** → doing the configuration *(Realization)*
> - **BC Sets** → recording what you configured *(Realization)*
> - **Transports** → moving that configuration to production *(Go Live)*
>
> **Transports and BC Sets are a pair** and worth understanding together: BC Sets **record** a configuration, Transports **carry** it from the development system to the live one. Together they are how a configuration made once is applied reliably everywhere — the ERP equivalent of a deployment pipeline, and the practical basis of ASAP's *re-usability* objective.

## Ascendant — PwC / IBM

> [!EXAM]
> **Ascendant is an integrated programme covering six workstreams:**
>
> **Project Management · Business · Organization · Application · Architecture · Operations**
>
> **These span all the phases of ERP implementation.**

> [!EXAM]
> **Ascendant's phase names** — the ones given as alternates in the life-cycle topic:
>
> **Evaluation → Project Preparation → Business blueprint → Realization → Final preparation → Go Live and support → Sustain**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Ascendant is a grid, not a sequence** — that is the whole difference from ASAP, and it is the point of the diagram.
>
> The **six workstreams run horizontally**; the **seven phases run vertically**. Every workstream is active in every phase, so at any moment the project is simultaneously doing project management, business work, organizational work, application work, architecture work and operations work.
>
> **Compare with the phase model**, which implies one thing happens at a time. Ascendant's claim is that **an ERP implementation has six parallel concerns throughout**, and that neglecting one — usually **Organization**, the change-management stream — is what kills projects.

> [!TRAP]
> **Ascendant has an explicit "Organization" workstream and a "Sustain" phase**, and both are things ASAP's five objectives do not name.
>
> That is a defensible exam contrast: **ASAP optimises for speed and reuse; Ascendant optimises for coverage and continuity.** Neither is wrong — they reflect their owners, a software vendor wanting fast go-lives versus a consultancy selling a long engagement.

## Why methodologies differ by project

> [!EXAM]
> Two question-bank items sit here:
> - **"Why does an ERP implementation need a methodology? Who can provide a methodology for implementation? Give examples of some popular methodologies."**
> - **"Why do different ERP projects need different types of methodologies?"**

> [!DERIVE]
> **The answer to the second is the types-of-projects list.** A **first-time implementation** and an **upgrade** are not the same work:
>
> | Project type | What the methodology must emphasise |
> |---|---|
> | **First-time implementation** | Blueprinting, BPR, change management — everything is new |
> | **Upgrade** | **Regression testing**, re-applying customizations — the processes already exist |
> | **Global roll-out** | **Template management** and country-specific localisation |
> | **Migration** | **Data conversion** above all |
> | **Consolidation / harmonization** | **Reconciling conflicting master data** across instances |
>
> A methodology built for a greenfield implementation spends months on As-Is modelling — **wasted effort on an upgrade, where the As-Is is the system you already run.** Hence: different projects, different methods.
