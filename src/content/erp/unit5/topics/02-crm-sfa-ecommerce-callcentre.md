# CRM Applications I — SFA, e-Commerce & Call Centre

## The application areas

> [!EXAM]
> **CRM Application Areas**, as the deck's diagram arranges them:
>
> **Sales Force Automation · Marketing · Field Service · e-Commerce · Call Centre · Partner Relationship Mgmt · Revenue and Pricing · Product Configuration · Order Management · CRM Analytics**
>
> **The list slide gives eight of them by name:**
> 1. **Sales Force Automation (SFA)**
> 2. **Field Service Applications**
> 3. **E-Commerce Applications**
> 4. **Call Center Applications**
> 5. **Partner Relationship Management**
> 6. **Marketing Management**
> 7. **Revenue and Pricing Management**
> 8. **Product Configuration Management**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Sort the ten by which stage of the funnel they serve and the set stops looking arbitrary:**
>
> | Funnel stage | Applications |
> |---|---|
> | **Find** | **Marketing Management** |
> | **Win** | **Sales Force Automation · Product Configuration · Revenue and Pricing · Order Management** |
> | **Serve and keep** | **Call Centre · Field Service** |
> | **Sell through others** | **Partner Relationship Management** |
> | **Sell without people** | **e-Commerce** |
> | **Understand all of it** | **CRM Analytics** |
>
> **Two of the ten are structurally different from the rest.** **PRM** is CRM applied to **channel partners rather than end customers** — a whole parallel relationship — and **e-Commerce** is the channel where **the customer does the selling to themselves.** Everything else supports a human employee doing something.

## Sales Force Automation

> [!EXAM]
> - **SFA applications help the sales force in their daily jobs and are aimed at increasing their productivity**
> - **With these applications, sales force need not remember or store contact information of thousands of the company's existing customers and new prospects**
> - **SFA applications help them to manage new leads and sales opportunities**
> - **These applications help sales force in managing sales in their territory and tracking sales performance, based on which they are provided incentive / compensation**
>
> **Typical areas of sales force automation applications:**
> 1. **Contact management**
> 2. **Lead management / Opportunity management**
> 3. **Sales forecasting and pipeline**
> 4. **Sales performance management**
> 5. **Territory management**
> 6. **Quote generation**
>
> **Web 2.0 is getting popularity among SFA vendors.**

> [!DERIVE]
> **Each SFA capability solves one specific problem a salesperson has**, and mapping them is how the list becomes reproducible:
>
> | Capability | The problem |
> |---|---|
> | **Contact management** | *"need not remember or store contact information of **thousands**"* |
> | **Lead / Opportunity management** | which of my many possible deals deserves my time today? |
> | **Sales forecasting and pipeline** | what will I actually close this quarter? |
> | **Territory management** | which accounts are mine, and which are a colleague's? |
> | **Sales performance management** | what did I achieve — **and therefore what am I paid?** |
> | **Quote generation** | produce a correct, priced proposal without a week of internal chasing |
>
> **The most important consequence is stated obliquely and is worth making explicit: *"tracking sales performance, based on which they are provided incentive / compensation."***
>
> **That is why sales people actually use the system.** An SFA that only helps management see the pipeline gets filled in reluctantly and badly. **An SFA that computes commission gets filled in accurately and on time** — because the salesperson has a direct financial stake in its correctness.
>
> **This is the classic adoption lesson**, and it echoes the Unit 4 failure factor *"user acceptance is critical — without that, even if the system goes LIVE, it will never be used by end users."* **Systems get used when the user, not the manager, benefits.**

> [!TRAP]
> **Territory management sounds administrative and is actually about conflict.**
>
> Without a defined territory, **two salespeople call the same prospect** — which wastes effort, confuses the customer, and produces an unresolvable argument about whose commission it is when the deal closes.
>
> **Territory management is therefore a *compensation* control as much as a coverage tool**, and it is why it sits next to sales performance management in the list.
>
> **And note the connection back to the funnel:** *lead management* and *opportunity management* are named as one capability because they are the **same object at two stages** — a qualified lead becomes an opportunity. **The pipeline is just the set of open opportunities, weighted by their probability of closing**, which is exactly how sales forecasting works.

## e-Commerce applications

> [!EXAM]
> - **These applications help companies to buy, sell, and do other transactions over the Web**
> - **Companies use these applications for: product search / browse · buying through shopping cart · product promotion · personalization · cross-sell and up-sell · product configurations · registries · multichannel ordering · customer self service**
> - **Today e-Commerce is becoming even more powerful with Web 2.0**
>
> **Typical capabilities of e-Commerce applications:**
> **eMarketing · Campaigns · Cross sell / Up sell · Personalization · Online ordering · Online product configuration · Online auctions · Online exchange and return · Online Billing and Payment**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Read the capability list and notice that it reimplements almost every other CRM application — without a human being.**
>
> | e-Commerce capability | Its staffed equivalent |
> |---|---|
> | **eMarketing, Campaigns** | **Marketing Management** |
> | **Cross sell / Up sell, Personalization** | what a **salesperson** would do |
> | **Online product configuration** | **Product Configuration Management** |
> | **Online ordering, billing and payment** | **Order Management** |
> | **Online exchange and return** | **Call Centre / Field Service** |
> | **Online auctions** | the **commodity procurement** tools, seen from the selling side |
>
> **e-Commerce is not a separate function — it is a separate *channel* through which all the functions are delivered.** That is why it can serve customers a sales force could never afford to visit: **the marginal cost of an additional online customer is nearly zero**, whereas the marginal cost of an additional visited customer is a salesperson's day.
>
> **"Personalization" is the piece that makes the channel competitive rather than merely cheap.** A shop assistant who remembers you is better than one who does not; **personalization is that memory, computed from the single customer view.**

## Call centre applications

> [!EXAM]
> - **These solutions provide customer support by answering different types of customer queries / calls and routing the calls to the most competent person who can handle it**
> - **Call centres today are capable of supporting complex customer interactions, such as scheduling service requests, providing technical support, and handling financial transactions**
>
> **Technologies a call centre uses:**
> **Local Area Networks (LAN) · Automatic Call Distributors (ACD) · Computer Technology Integration (CTI) · web integration · Interactive Voice Response (IVR) · voice logging and messaging · IVR / speech portals**

> [!EXAM]
> **Typical capabilities of call centre applications — the question bank asks for exactly this list, and the QnA answers it:**
>
> 1. **Predefined escalation route**
> 2. **Previous records of the caller**
> 3. **Support in different time zones in different languages**
> 4. **Managing call assignments**
> 5. **Knowledge base**
> 6. **Self service capabilities**
> 7. **Workflow**
> 8. **Field service management**

> [!DERIVE]
> **The eight capabilities answer four questions about a call, in the order they arise:**
>
> | Question | Capabilities |
> |---|---|
> | **Who should take this call?** | **ACD**, **managing call assignments**, **predefined escalation route** |
> | **Who is calling, and what happened before?** | **previous records of the caller** *(delivered by **CTI** — the caller's record opens as the phone rings)* |
> | **How does the agent solve it?** | **knowledge base**, **workflow** |
> | **Does it need to go further?** | **field service management** — dispatch an engineer |
>
> **"Previous records of the caller" is the capability the question bank singles out**, and its value is easy to state: without it, **every call starts from zero** — the customer re-explains their problem to a third person, and the company appears not to be one company. **It is the single customer view, arriving in the two seconds before the agent says hello.** That is what **CTI (Computer Technology Integration)** exists to do: link the telephone system to the customer database so the screen and the call arrive together.
>
> **"Predefined escalation route" prevents the other classic failure** — an agent who cannot solve a problem and does not know who can. Escalation defined **in advance** means the path is a rule, not an improvisation.
>
> **And the knowledge base is what makes self-service possible at all.** The question bank asks *"how does the call centre knowledge base help in better problem solving for customers?"* — the answer being that **once a solution is written down, it can be reused by any agent, and then exposed directly to customers** so they never need to call.
>
> $$\textbf{one agent solves it} \rightarrow \textbf{knowledge base} \rightarrow \textbf{every agent solves it} \rightarrow \textbf{self-service: the customer solves it}$$
>
> **Each arrow removes cost and reduces waiting.** That is the whole economic logic of a modern contact centre, and it is the same *"move the transaction to the person who has the need"* principle as **ESS** in HCM and **employee e-procurement** for indirect items.

> [!TRAP]
> **"Support in different time zones in different languages" and "field service management" are the two that look out of place on a call-centre list.**
>
> **Time zones and languages** are there because the call centre is often **offshored** — which is the same **ODC / GDC** model as ERP support in Unit 4, where *"support"* carried the **highest offshore percentage at 80%** precisely because it is ticket-driven and time-zone difference becomes an advantage.
>
> **Field service management** is on the list because **the call is where a service visit is born.** A customer reports a fault by phone; if it cannot be fixed remotely, an engineer must be scheduled. **The call centre and field service are two ends of one process**, which is why the next topic treats field service in its own right.
