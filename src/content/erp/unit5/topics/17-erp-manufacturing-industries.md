# ERP for Manufacturing Industries

## Common versus industry-specific processes

> [!EXAM]
> **Examples of common processes across industries:**
> - **Financial accounting**
> - **Management accounting**
> - **Corporate governance**
> - **Human resource management processes** like **recruitment, payroll processing, talent management**
> - **Corporate services** like **travel management**
> - **Quality management**
> - **Processes of global trade** — **import procedure, documentation, customs**
> - **Managing some of the logistics functions** like **warehouse and transport management**
>
> **Examples of processes that differ across industries:**
> > *"An **oil and gas company** needs capabilities around **oil exploration** for finding new oil reserves — but that is not something important for a consumer goods company. In the same way, **product promotion** is an important feature for a consumer goods company to boost sales, however that may not make any sense for a petroleum company where prices in countries like India are mostly dictated by government."*

> [!INTUITION]
> **The dividing line is precise, and naming it is the answer to why industry solutions exist at all.**
>
> $$\textbf{common} = \textbf{how a company runs itself} \qquad\qquad \textbf{specific} = \textbf{what the company actually does}$$
>
> **Every company pays people, keeps accounts, complies with law and moves goods** — so **FI, CO, HR and basic logistics are the same everywhere.** That is exactly the set Unit 3 called *"across the whole enterprise"* — **money and people are nobody's exclusive property.**
>
> **The core operating process is where industries diverge**, and the deck's own example pair is well chosen: **exploration** matters only upstream; **promotion** matters only where you set your own prices. **A petroleum company in India cannot promote its way to more sales because the government fixes the price** — so the feature is not merely unused, it is meaningless.
>
> **That is the commercial basis of the whole industry-solution market**, and it is what makes **"industry specific functionality"** capable of overriding an entire package-selection process in Unit 4.

## Oil and gas

> [!EXAM]
> **Industry-specific processes:**
> - **Exploration**
> - **Developing petroleum fields**
> - **Managing refinery operations**
> - **Lubes manufacturing operations**
> - **Oil and gas commodity trading**
> - **Managing bulk transport of inflammable materials and hydrocarbon products**
> - **Managing terminals management**
> - **Managing service station operations / fuel management**
> - **Convenience retailing**
>
> **Oil industry specific ERPs: Aspentech · SAP IS-Oil**

> [!EXAM]
> **The oil and gas value chain**, as the deck's diagram draws it:
>
> $$\textbf{Upstream} \rightarrow \textbf{Mid-Stream} \rightarrow \textbf{Refining} \rightarrow \textbf{Primary Distribution Network} \rightarrow \textbf{Secondary Distribution Network} \rightarrow \textbf{Service Station Retailing}$$
>
> with the product streams spanning different segments: **Crude** *(upstream → refining)* · **Refined Products & LPG** *(refining → retail)* · **Lubes** · **Natural Gas**
>
> **The integrated supply chain slide expands it:**
> **Crude Oil / NG → Refinery → Pipelines → Terminal → Station → Industrial Customer / Reseller**, with the activities **Select / Acquire Crude → Refine Crude → Optimize Product Mix → Move Products → Store Products → Replenish Products → Sell Products**, across the functions **Supply | Bulk Distribution | Terminalling | Retail Distribution.**

> [!DERIVE]
> **The single most distinctive thing about oil is that one company spans the whole chain from a hole in the ground to a petrol pump** — and the process list reads that chain end to end:
>
> | Value chain stage | Process |
> |---|---|
> | **Upstream** | **exploration · developing petroleum fields** |
> | **Refining** | **managing refinery operations · lubes manufacturing** |
> | **Distribution** | **bulk transport of inflammable materials · terminals management** |
> | **Retail** | **service station operations / fuel management · convenience retailing** |
> | **Throughout** | **oil and gas commodity trading** |
>
> **"Optimize Product Mix" is the process that has no equivalent in discrete manufacturing.** A refinery does not choose to make petrol *or* diesel — **one barrel of crude yields a whole slate of products simultaneously**, and the optimisation is choosing **which slate** to run given current prices for every output.
>
> **That is why Aspentech — described earlier in this unit as *"strong in Process Industry"* — is named here.** It is also why **commodity trading** is on the list: crude and refined products are **traded commodities**, so **hedging** (from Unit 4's commodity procurement) is core business rather than a procurement technique.
>
> **And "convenience retailing" is the entry that shows how far the chain reaches** — the same ERP that models a reservoir also has to run a shop selling crisps and coffee. **SAP's twenty-five industry solutions exist because a single generic package cannot span that.**

> [!EXAM]
> **SAP Oil & Gas Solution Map** — the deck reproduces SAP's own map, structured as:
>
> | Area | Contents |
> |---|---|
> | **Enterprise Management** | Strategic Enterprise Management · Business Analytics · Business Intelligence & Decision Support · Accounting · Workforce Planning & Alignment · **Joint Venture Management** |
> | **Customer Relationship Management** | Customer Engagement · Business Transaction · Order Fulfilment · Customer Service |
> | **Upstream** | **Exploration & Appraisal · Development · Production · Marketing · Disposal** |
> | **Supply** | Supply Chain Optimization & Planning · **Acquire, Trade & Sell** · **Exchange & Throughput Handling** · Scheduling · Inventory Management · Primary Distribution & Transport |
> | **Manufacturing** | Manufacturing Planning & Optimization · Manufacturing Process · **Batch Manufacturing** · **Blending & Packaging** · Product Quality Management |
> | **Market** | Marketing · Contracts & Pricing · Commercial Sales · Secondary Distribution & Transportation |
> | **Service Station & Convenience Retailing** | Category Management · Transaction Processing & Settlement · Retail Network Management · Inventory Management · Site Management |
> | **Business Support** | HR Operations · Sourcing & Deployment · Procurement · Financial Supply Chain Management · Treasury / Corporate Finance · Fixed Asset Management · **Engineering, Construction & Maintenance** |

> [!TRAP]
> **Two entries on that map are genuinely industry-exclusive and worth naming.**
>
> **"Joint Venture Management"** — oil fields are **too expensive and too risky for one company**, so they are developed by consortia. That means **costs and revenues must be split among partners** according to their share, with statements issued to each. **No generic ERP has this**, and it is one of the strongest reasons an oil company cannot use a standard package.
>
> **"Exchange & Throughput Handling"** — oil companies routinely **swap product with competitors** rather than transport it. *You give me a tanker in Chennai; I give you one in Mumbai.* **Both save the freight.** These exchanges must be tracked and settled, and it is a transaction type that exists in essentially no other industry.
>
> **And note that "Business Support" is exactly the common-process list from the start of this topic** — HR, procurement, FSCM, treasury, fixed assets. **The map visually separates what is universal from what is industry-exclusive**, which is the whole point of the topic.

## Automotive

> [!EXAM]
> **Industry-specific processes:**
> - **Kanban planning / scheduling for multiple models in an assembly line**
> - **Warranty Management**
> - **Just in sequence deliveries by suppliers directly to assembly line**
> - **Managing Vehicle information and Vehicle life cycle**
> - **Managing Dealer Business**
> - **Car Leasing**

> [!INTUITION]
> **"Just in sequence" is Just-in-Time taken one step further, and the distinction is examinable.**
>
> $$\textbf{JIT: the right parts arrive at the right time} \qquad \textbf{JIS: the right parts arrive } \textit{in the order the line will use them}$$
>
> On a line building **multiple models mixed together**, the seats for car 47 must arrive **immediately before car 47 reaches the seat station** — not a pallet of assorted seats. **The supplier must sequence their delivery to your production sequence**, which requires them to see your line schedule in near real time.
>
> **That is the deepest possible supply-chain collaboration** — beyond VMI, beyond CPFR — and it is why the automotive industry drove so much of this technology. **Toyota gave this course Kanban and JIT in Unit 3**; JIS is the same idea with sequence added.
>
> **And "managing vehicle information and vehicle life cycle" is distinctive because the individual unit matters.** Most manufacturers track a *product*; a car maker tracks **this specific car** — by VIN, through manufacture, sale, service, warranty claims and resale. **That is why warranty management and dealer business are on the same list**: they are all operations on an individual, identified, long-lived unit.
>
> **"Car leasing" is the entry that shows the industry becoming a financial services business too** — the manufacturer finances the purchase, so the ERP must handle a lease portfolio.

## Pharmaceutical

> [!EXAM]
> **Industry-specific processes:**
> - **Patent Management**
> - **Managing Intellectual Property**
> - **Clinical trial management**
> - **Laboratory Information Management Systems (LIMS)**
> - **Secure Tracing and Tracking / E-Pedigree**
> - **Product / Patient Safety Regulatory Compliance Submission**
> - **Secrecy of Formulation**

> [!DERIVE]
> **Every process on this list exists because of one of two pressures: *regulation* or *intellectual property*.**
>
> | Pressure | Processes |
> |---|---|
> | **Regulation** | clinical trial management · LIMS · **E-Pedigree** · regulatory compliance submission |
> | **Intellectual property** | patent management · managing IP · **secrecy of formulation** |
>
> **E-Pedigree is the sharpest of them:** an electronic record of **every custody transfer a drug pack has undergone**, from manufacturer to pharmacy. It exists to defeat **counterfeit medicines** entering the legitimate supply chain — and it is a legal requirement, not a business choice.
>
> **That is batch management from Unit 3 taken to its logical end.** Batch tracking answered *"which batch is this?"*; e-pedigree answers *"who has held this specific pack, and when?"* — the same **Farm to Fork** traceability, at pharmaceutical stakes.
>
> **"Secrecy of formulation" is the one that constrains the ERP's own design.** In an ordinary company, the BOM is visible to planners, buyers and costing. **In pharma the formulation *is* the asset**, so the system must let people plan and buy against a recipe **without letting them see it** — which is the material-master access principle from Unit 3 (*"a purchase clerk might have access to sensitive information… but the works manager may not"*) enforced at maximum strictness.
>
> **And LIMS is a named interface in Unit 4's interface-scope list** — *"interface with Laboratory Management Information Systems."* **The instruments produce the test results; the ERP cannot release the batch without them.**

## Consumer goods

> [!EXAM]
> **Industry-specific processes:**
> - **Strong distribution planning capability**
> - **Promotion Planning / Trade Promotions**
> - **Brand management**
> - **Managing large number of SKUs**
> - **Manufacturing outsourcing / Contract manufacturing**
> - **Collaboration with Retailers**
> - **Route management**
> - **Direct Store Delivery (DSD)**
> - **Food Safety**
> - **Managing Cold Chain** *(mainly for fresh foods)*
> - **Managing Empties and returns** *(mainly for beverage)*
> - **Markdowns** *(mainly for apparel industry)*
> - **Managing Fashion, Style, Design** *(mainly for apparel industry)*

> [!INTUITION]
> **This list is the mineral-water question answered.** For consumer goods, **the product is cheap and the supply chain is everything** — which is why nine of the thirteen processes are distribution, logistics or retail collaboration.
>
> | Theme | Processes |
> |---|---|
> | **Getting it to the shelf** | distribution planning · route management · **DSD** · cold chain · empties and returns |
> | **Making it sell once there** | promotion planning / trade promotions · brand management · markdowns · fashion, style, design |
> | **Working with the retailer** | **collaboration with retailers** |
> | **Scale and structure** | large number of SKUs · contract manufacturing |
>
> **Direct Store Delivery is the distinctive one:** the manufacturer's own vehicle delivers **straight to each shop**, bypassing the retailer's distribution centre — used for products that are **perishable, high-frequency or need shelf servicing** (bread, soft drinks, snacks). **It gives the manufacturer control of the shelf and costs them a delivery fleet**, which is why "route management" sits right beside it.
>
> **"Collaboration with retailers" is where this unit's own VMI and CPFR live** — **Wal-Mart and P&G** was precisely a consumer-goods-to-retailer collaboration, and **CPFR** was a retailer's initiative. **This industry is the reason those techniques exist.**
>
> **And "managing empties and returns" is a reverse-logistics flow** of exactly the kind **SCOR's *Return*** process models: bottles and crates come **back**, must be tracked as assets, and re-enter the chain.

## Mining

> [!EXAM]
> **Industry-specific processes:**
> - **Safety**
> - **Handling hazardous materials (Explosives)**
> - **Managing logistics at remote locations**
> - **Transport optimization**
> - **Washing / Beneficiation**
> - **Blending**
> - **Commodity pricing**
> - **Commodity auctions**
> - **Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)**
> - **Exploration**
>
> **Mining-specific ERPs: SAP IS-Mine · Mincom · Gemcom**

> [!TRAP]
> **Two entries on the mining list are unusual enough to be worth remembering.**
>
> **"Washing / Beneficiation"** is the process of **raising the grade of extracted ore** by removing waste rock. It is genuinely peculiar from an ERP standpoint because **the input is variable in quality and the output quantity is not fixed** — you cannot write a BOM that says *one tonne of ore yields X tonnes of concentrate*, because it depends on the ore. **Blending then combines ores of different grades to hit a target specification.** Both are **process-industry recipes with variable yield**, which no discrete BOM can express.
>
> **"Corporate Social Responsibility"** appearing as a *process* is striking. Mining operates in remote communities and under intense environmental and social scrutiny; **CSR commitments — local employment, community investment, rehabilitation — are contractual conditions of the mining licence**, so they must be tracked and reported like any other obligation. **It is compliance, not philanthropy.**
>
> **Note that Mincom was named in Unit 4** as an example of a package *"strong in a particular industry"* — **Mincom in Mining.** This is the same fact from the other direction, and it is why *"industry specific functionality"* was listed as a legitimate reason to bypass the standard package-selection process.
