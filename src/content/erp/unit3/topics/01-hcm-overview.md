# HCM — Overview & Areas

## The changing scenario

> [!EXAM]
> - **Traditionally most of the tasks were completely owned by the HR department**
> - **Nowadays, line managers have lots of responsibilities towards their team members** — especially in **knowledge based industries**. Times are changing even in traditional companies with respect to HR practices
> - **So, the HR "function" is distributed — it is essential to have an integrating system for smooth functioning across all processes!**
> - **The HR module is one of the late entrants in the ERP suite**

> [!INTUITION]
> The deck's explanation of *why* the function became distributed is the useful part:
>
> > *"A design department manager only knows and can assess the performance of a design engineer and recommend to HR. **HR does not have the ability to measure performance in certain areas**, neither does the employee trust a fair treatment if handled only by HR."*
>
> So the shift is driven by **where the knowledge is**. In a knowledge industry, only the line manager can judge the work — HR cannot. That splits the HR function across hundreds of managers who are **not HR professionals and do not sit in one place.**
>
> **A distributed function needs an integrating system.** That is the argument for HCM in one line, and it is the *from department to enterprise* driver of Unit 1 applied inside HR.

> [!TRAP]
> **"The HR module is one of the late entrants in the ERP suite"** is worth knowing and explaining.
>
> ERP's lineage is **MRP → MRP II → ERP** — a manufacturing lineage, where the early modules were materials, production and then finance. **People were the last resource to be modelled**, even though HCM sits under the **"Resource"** in *Enterprise Resource Planning*.
>
> This also explains why so many **specialist HCM vendors** exist alongside the ERP majors: the gap was open long enough for others to fill it.

## The ten HCM areas

> [!EXAM]
> The areas on the deck's HCM wheel:
>
> 1. **Recruitment**
> 2. **Personnel Management**
> 3. **Time, Leave, Attendance**
> 4. **Benefits and Payroll**
> 5. **Compensation Management**
> 6. **Talent / Performance Management**
> 7. **Learning Management**
> 8. **Workforce Scheduling**
> 9. **Employee Self Service**
> 10. **Employee Health & Safety (EHS)**
>
> The deck labels the whole wheel with the process it implements: **the Hire-to-Retire process.**

> [!INTUITION]
> **Mnemonic — walk the employee's life through the wheel.** The ten areas are not a random list; they are **H2R in order**:
>
> | Stage of the employee's life | Area |
> |---|---|
> | Before they join | **Recruitment** |
> | The day they join | **Personnel Management** *(the record)* |
> | Every day thereafter | **Time, Leave, Attendance** · **Workforce Scheduling** |
> | Every month | **Benefits and Payroll** · **Compensation Management** |
> | Every year | **Talent / Performance Management** · **Learning Management** |
> | Throughout | **Employee Self Service** · **Employee Health & Safety** |
>
> **Recruit → record → schedule → pay → develop**, with **ESS** as the channel the employee uses and **EHS** as the duty of care running underneath.
>
> Recall from Unit 1 that **H2R (Hire to Retire)** was one of the six named business processes: *Recruitment → Hiring → Onboarding → Training → Performance Management → Payroll → Promotion → Retirement/Exit.* **HCM is the module that implements it.**

## The order of automation

> [!EXAM]
> **Typical sequence of adopting ERP for HR functions and processes:**
>
> **1) Transaction-intensive HR processes are typically automated first:**
> - **Payroll**
> - **Employee record management**
> - **Time and attendance management**
> - **Leave management**
>
> **2) Recruitment processes are next to be automated:**
> - **Sourcing · Assessments · Offer · Onboarding**
>
> **3) Strategic HR processes are typically automated last:**
> - **Talent Management · Performance Management · Learning Management**

> [!EXAM]
> The deck's fuller breakdown of the three waves:
>
> | Wave | Contents |
> |---|---|
> | **Transaction Intensive Processes** | Managing employee record · Time and attendance · **Payroll** · Absence management · Benefits administration |
> | **Recruitment Processes** | HR planning / labor forecast · Tracking applications · Recruitment · Employee onboarding · Job portals integration |
> | **Performance, Compensation, Talent Mgmt** | Career planning · Compensation / reward management · Employee performance mgmt · **Succession planning** · Managing talents |
> | **Employee Learning** | Training administration · Managing/developing training content · Online learning · Individual employee learning plan · **Knowledge management / building learning organization** |

> [!DERIVE]
> **The deck asks the question directly — "Reason for this order?"** — so be ready to answer it.
>
> The order follows **return on automation effort**, and three properties explain it:
>
> | | Transaction-intensive | Strategic |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Volume** | Very high — every employee, every month | Low — once or twice a year |
> | **Rules** | **Well-defined and objective** — hours × rate, leave balances | **Judgement-based and subjective** — is this person promotable? |
> | **Cost of error** | Immediate and visible — a wrong payslip | Diffuse and slow |
>
> **Automate the high-volume, rule-governed, error-prone work first.** Payroll is the extreme case: it runs monthly for everyone, the rules are arithmetic, and mistakes are noticed instantly.
>
> **Strategic processes resist automation** because the underlying judgement cannot be encoded — a system can *record* a performance rating and *route* it for approval, but it cannot *form* the opinion. So the software adds proportionally less, and it goes last.

> [!TRAP]
> This ordering is exactly what the **strategic vs operational HR** classification later formalises — and the MCQ bank tests both.
>
> Note the direction of the trap: **payroll is operational/routine, not strategic**; **planning job roles is strategic, not operational.** The pasted MCQs ask both.
