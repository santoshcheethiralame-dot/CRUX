# Financial SCM & Treasury

## Financial Supply Chain Management

> [!EXAM]
> **Areas of Financial Supply Chain Management (FSCM):**
>
> 1. **Collection Management**
> 2. **Credit Management**
> 3. **Dispute Management**
> 4. **Self Service Portals**
>
> Plus **Management Reports and Dashboards.**
>
> *(The QnA's short answer names the first three.)*

> [!INTUITION]
> **Mnemonic — FSCM is the three C's: Collection, Credit, Dispute** — or *"can they pay, will they pay, why haven't they paid?"*
>
> | Area | The question | When it acts |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Credit Management** | *Can they pay?* | **before** the sale — set a credit limit |
> | **Collection Management** | *Will they pay?* | **after** the invoice — chase it |
> | **Dispute Management** | *Why haven't they paid?* | when payment **stops** for a reason |
>
> Reading them in time order is what makes the set memorable: **credit comes first, collection second, disputes third** — you only get to a dispute after you have failed to collect.

> [!DERIVE]
> **Why is it called a "supply chain" at all?**
>
> Because it mirrors the physical supply chain with **money flowing the other way**. Goods move outward to the customer; **cash moves back**, and that return flow has its own delays, blockages and risks — exactly like a supply chain.
>
> This is the **Order-to-Cash** cycle's tail end from Unit 1: stages 5–7 were *Invoicing → Payment Receipt → Cash Application*, and the deck was explicit that Q2C **does not end at delivery**. **FSCM is the module that manages those last three stages.**
>
> And it is what produces the tangible benefit **"reducing number of days outstanding"** — *accurate and timely invoicing (fewer disputes), tracking of due and overdue invoices, reminders and **dunning**, and linking to order entry to manage defaulting customers.*

> [!TRAP]
> **Dispute Management is the area students overlook, and it is the interesting one.**
>
> An unpaid invoice has two very different causes: the customer **cannot or will not pay** (a collection problem), or the customer **believes the invoice is wrong** (a dispute). Chasing a disputed invoice with reminders is useless and damages the relationship — you have to **resolve the disagreement** instead.
>
> Separating the two is what lets collection effort go where it will work. Note the connection: *"accurate and timely invoicing — **less disputes**"* means **the best dispute management is not generating disputes in the first place**, which comes from the integration that makes invoices correct.

> [!EXAM]
> **Self Service Portals** here are **customer**-facing — the mirror of the employee portal in HCM. They let the customer see their own account, invoices and payment status, which reduces enquiry volume and resolves questions before they become disputes.

## Treasury

> [!EXAM]
> **Treasury Management areas — the three:**
>
> 1. **Cash and Liquidity Management**
> 2. **Financial Risk Management**
> 3. **Managing Bank Interactions**
>
> From the application categories: **Treasury** — *"Bank/FI transaction management"* — and **Cash Management** — *"cash flow, investments, liquidity, financial risks."*

> [!INTUITION]
> **Treasury is about the money the company holds, as opposed to the money it is owed.**
>
> - **Cash and liquidity** — *do we have enough cash, in the right place, at the right time?* A profitable company can still fail if the cash arrives after the salaries are due. **Liquidity is a timing problem, not a profit problem.**
> - **Financial risk** — **currency, interest rate and counterparty** exposure. Recall the AR benefit from Unit 1: *"reduces risks of **currency fluctuation** impacts in cross-border transactions."* That is treasury risk management touching receivables.
> - **Bank interactions** — a large group deals with many banks in many countries; treasury is where those relationships and transactions are managed centrally.

> [!DERIVE]
> **Treasury is where the "Multi" problems bite hardest in Finance**, and it is worth saying so:
>
> - **Multi-country** → accounts in many jurisdictions, each with its own banking rules
> - **Multi-currency** → the **Foreign Exchange Management** function shown in the financial accounting diagram
> - **Multi-division** → cash sitting idle in one subsidiary while another borrows
>
> That last case is the clearest argument for centralised treasury: **without visibility across entities, a group can be paying interest on a loan in one country while holding surplus cash in another.** Only a consolidated view makes that visible — Unit 1's *unified view & synergy across the enterprise*, in cash.

## The four categories, once more

> [!EXAM]
> The chapter summary states the whole structure compactly:
>
> > **ERP FM supports the traditional functional areas of finance:**
> > - **Financial accounting** — **AR, AP, GL, Tax accounting, Inventory accounting**
> > - **Management accounting** — **Profit & Cost Centre accounting, Product Cost & Project accounting**
> > - **Financial SCM** — **Collection, Credit and Debtors management**
> > - **Treasury applications** — **Cash, Liquidity and Risk management**
> >
> > **FM is a highly integrated ERP module** — other areas of ERP transactions have financial implications.
> > **Emerging areas of FM are budgeting, consolidation, ABCs, Business Performance Management.**

> [!INTUITION]
> Note the summary calls FSCM **"Collection, Credit and **Debtors** management"** — *debtors* being the accounting word for customers who owe you money. Unit 1's ROI worked example used the same term: *"**Debtors** — track regularly — dunning — outstanding 50 days → 45 days."*
>
> **That 5-day reduction was worth 1.62 Cr in the business case, and FSCM is the module that delivers it.**
